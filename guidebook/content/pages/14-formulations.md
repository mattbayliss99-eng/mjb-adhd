@layer 1 Start with the product and the benefit
If a prescribed medicine feels weak, inconsistent or as though it does nothing, the explanation may be that it does not suit you. But useful questions also concern what was supplied, how it delivers the active drug, when you assessed it, interactions and the function you wanted to improve. A lack of benefit alone does not establish an allergy, poor absorption or a defective tablet.

**Same active ingredient. Different tablet. Potentially a different experience.** Record an apparent difference precisely, then ask a pharmacist and prescriber to review it.

!patient(AUTHOR-ACCOUNT) Looking back at my earlier treatment, I experienced much better everyday function with Amfexa 10 mg and 20 mg tablets than with the generic dexamfetamine 5 mg tablets I had previously been given. I rarely collected the earlier product because I experienced little useful benefit. This is my retrospective account; it is not a controlled comparison or a recommendation for somebody else.

!hypothesis() I wondered whether povidone or a related ingredient contributed to that difference. I have not isolated the ingredient from the other differences between products and treatment periods. This remains a question, not a demonstrated mechanism or an allergy diagnosis — see [[open-questions]].

@layer 2 What else could change the experience?
Product differences deserve consideration alongside dose, time course, sleep, pain, other medicines and daily demands. More than one factor may be involved.

| Question | Information that helps a review |
|---|---|
| Was the same active drug, strength and dose supplied? | Box, pharmacy label, manufacturer, strength and dispensing record |
| Was the release system different? | Immediate or modified release; when benefit began and ended |
| Did timing, food or another medicine change? | A simple dated account, rather than guessing from memory |
| Was the desired benefit the same? | Initiation, attention, persistence, motor function and unwanted effects recorded separately |
| Did sleep, pain or the task itself change? | What else was different on the relevant days |

An improvement in concentration is not automatically an improvement in every other symptom. Equally, a day with little useful function does not directly measure the drug's blood concentration. Retrospective impressions are valuable starting points; repeated observations and dispensing records can help test alternative explanations.

@layer 3 Check the pack and ask a specific question
1. Write down the **exact name, manufacturer, strength and dosage form**. Include the product licence number (PL) if available. Different strengths of one brand can have different ingredients.
2. Read the product's Patient Information Leaflet. Its contents section lists the other ingredients. In the clinician-facing Summary of Product Characteristics, look at section **6.1: List of excipients**.
3. Record what changed and when: product, prescribed dose, time taken, meals, other medicines, expected benefit, observed benefit, duration and adverse effects.
4. Ask your pharmacist to confirm which product was dispensed. Ask your prescriber whether a formulation change, interaction review or another clinical assessment is appropriate.

[Find UK product information on emc](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/). Bring a photograph of the pack or pharmacy label if remembering its name is difficult.

> [!warn] Keep any comparison clinician-led
> Do not change doses, stop treatment or crush, split or dissolve a medicine to test an explanation unless your pharmacist or prescriber confirms that the specific change is appropriate. Product score lines and release properties differ.

@layer 4 What excipients do — and what the lists show
An excipient is a medicine ingredient other than the active substance, included for a particular purpose. Fillers, binders, disintegrants, lubricants, coatings and colourants are examples. Some ingredients have recognised effects or tolerability implications; an ingredient's presence does not make the product inferior. {EXC-EMA}

!institution(EXC-EMA) European regulatory information recognises that some excipients can have effects in particular circumstances and requires corresponding ingredient and safety information. This supports checking the actual formulation; it does not demonstrate the cause of one person's poor response.

The following comparison uses the named UK manufacturer documents checked on 14 October 2026. It concerns ingredient lists, not comparative efficacy.

| Named product | Listed excipients | Product information |
|---|---|---|
| Amfexa / Dexamfetamine Sulfate Medice 5 mg | Isomalt, crospovidone, magnesium stearate | {EXC-A5} |
| Amfexa / Dexamfetamine Sulfate Medice 10 mg | Isomalt, magnesium stearate, yellow iron oxide | {EXC-A10} |
| Amfexa / Dexamfetamine Sulfate Medice 20 mg | Isomalt, magnesium stearate, red iron oxide | {EXC-A20} |
| Brown & Burk dexamfetamine sulfate 5 mg | Microcrystalline cellulose, calcium hydrogen phosphate dihydrate, povidone, maize starch, magnesium stearate | {EXC-BB} |

!guidance(EXC-A5,EXC-A10,EXC-A20,EXC-BB) These documents list different excipients in tablets containing dexamfetamine sulfate. They do not establish that a particular excipient causes reduced benefit or that one tablet is more effective.

Povidone and crospovidone are related but distinct ingredients. Do not use their names interchangeably. The manufacturer of the earlier generic product in my personal account has not been established here; the Brown & Burk entry is a verified example, not an identification of what I was dispensed.

Some local NHS agreements advise prescribing dexamfetamine by brand. That is a local prescribing instruction, not proof of a universal clinical difference. {P1}

@layer 5 Elvanse has a different delivery route
Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug: the body converts it into active dexamfetamine. The product information identifies blood, primarily red blood cells, as the main conversion setting. It also describes substantial conversion capacity in laboratory measurements. {EXC-LDX}

!guidance(EXC-LDX) Poor real-world benefit does not by itself demonstrate failed lisdexamfetamine activation. A subjective effect, a measured blood concentration and everyday functional capacity answer different questions.

The established conversion route does not support an assertion that an energetically inefficient nervous system necessarily makes Elvanse break down unpredictably. A person can still experience incomplete or inconsistent benefit that deserves clinical review. The uncertainty belongs in the explanation, not in whether the experience is worth listening to.

@layer 6 A record you can bring to a review
Keep this small enough to use: **product and strength; what changed; time taken; function improved or not improved; unwanted effects; what else was different.** Dates and a photograph of the pack can be more useful than a long account reconstructed later.

If useful, add the PL number, time of first noticeable benefit, duration, sleep and meals. Record contradictory days as well as days that fit an explanation.

For suspected adverse reactions, the [MHRA Yellow Card scheme](https://yellowcard.mhra.gov.uk/) accepts reports. If there is facial or throat swelling, difficulty breathing or collapse, seek emergency help rather than trying the product again yourself.

@uncertainty
An ingredient-list comparison identifies formulation differences. It does not diagnose hypersensitivity, establish altered absorption or prove why a medicine helped one person less. A poor response should be evaluated without treating every proposed mechanism as established.
