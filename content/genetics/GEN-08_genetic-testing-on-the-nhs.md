# Genetic testing on the NHS: what to ask for and what results can mean

**Kicker:** Science / Practical
**Last updated:** 30 September 2026

## The short version

- NHS genetic tests are requested by a clinician, usually in neurology or clinical genetics, and matched to a national list of approved tests and the situations they are approved for (the National Genomic Test Directory; HOLD: confirm current version).
- ADHD alone is not a standard reason for a genetic test (HOLD). Unexplained movement symptoms, family history or developmental features may be.
- A result falls into one of five classes. Only two of them are diagnoses. One of them, "uncertain", is very common and is **not** a diagnosis.

## The usual mix-up

"A normal gene test means nothing is wrong" and "an abnormal gene test means this explains everything" are both wrong. A test looks only at the genes in the panel, and a result has to be interpreted against the person's symptoms.

## The five result classes

These follow the standard classification used by clinical genetics laboratories (ACMG/AMP 2015 framework; HOLD: cite the paper).

| Class | In plain English | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| Pathogenic | Known to cause disease | Usually counts as a genetic diagnosis if it fits the symptoms |
| Likely pathogenic | Very probably causes disease | Usually treated as a diagnosis if it fits |
| Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) | Not enough evidence either way | **Not a diagnosis.** Should be reviewed, sometimes with family testing, and revisited as evidence grows |
| Likely benign | Very probably harmless | Normally not reported as a cause |
| Benign | Harmless | Normally not reported |

## What to ask when a test is requested

1. What is the **exact name** of the test, and which genes does it cover?
2. What is its code in the National Genomic Test Directory?
3. Which laboratory will run it, and what is its normal turnaround time?
4. Who will receive the result, and who will explain it to me?
5. What happens if the result is a VUS? Who reviews it, and when?
6. What will the result change about my care?
7. What does consent cover, including unrelated findings and family implications?

## If a sample is lost or a result is overdue

Record these in writing and keep a copy:

- Date the sample was taken, where, and by whom.
- Name of the test and the laboratory reference, if you have one.
- The date you were told to expect a result.
- Who owns the follow-up, by name and role.
- A written date for any re-sample.

If the answer to "who owns this?" is unclear, ask for it to be confirmed in writing. A service that cannot say who holds the next step has a governance gap that can be raised through the provider's complaints route (see the rights section).

## Public resources that help you read a result

- **GeneReviews**: clinician-written summaries of single-gene conditions.
- **OMIM**: catalogue of genes and inherited conditions.
- **ClinVar**: database of variants and how laboratories have classified them.
- **Orphanet**: rare-disease information.

These help you ask better questions. They do not replace an interpretation from a clinician who has your results and your symptoms.

## Direct-to-consumer tests

Commercial DNA kits are not a substitute for a clinical test. They look at a limited set of variants, may produce false positives for rare variants, and are not validated for diagnosing the conditions discussed in this series (standard position; HOLD for specifics).

## Privacy

Genetic data is treated as special category data under UK data-protection law (HOLD: cite source). Ask who can see results and whether they will be shared with other services.
