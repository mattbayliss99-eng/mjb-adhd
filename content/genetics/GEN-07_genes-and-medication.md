# Genes and medication

**Kicker:** Confirmed science / Genetics and treatment
**Last updated:** 30 September 2026

## The short version

- **Pharmacogenomics** studies how your genes change the way your body handles a drug.
- For ADHD it is established for some non-stimulants and is **not** routine for stimulants.
- A medication response is not a diagnostic test, and a gene result is not an instruction to stop, withhold or swap a treatment.

## The usual mix-up

"It works for you, so you must have ADHD" and "your DNA says you will not respond" are both overreach. Response to a drug tells a clinician something about that drug in that person. It does not prove or disprove a diagnosis, and no validated genetic test currently tells a clinician who will respond to a particular ADHD medicine (HOLD: confirm against current guidance).

## The plain-English layer

Four things decide what a drug does in a body: how it is **absorbed**, how it is **changed or cleared** (mostly by liver enzymes), how it is **moved** across membranes, and what it **binds to**. Genes can alter each.

| Drug | How the body handles it (standard pharmacology) | Genetic angle | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Atomoxetine** | Cleared mainly by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 | People with little or no CYP2D6 activity ("poor metabolisers") have higher drug exposure | A CPIC guideline exists (HOLD: confirm current version at cpicpgx.org). Prescribers also use the product information |
| **Methylphenidate** | Broken down mainly by an enzyme called carboxylesterase 1 (CES1) | Variants in CES1 are under study | No routine testing (HOLD) |
| **Lisdexamfetamine** | A prodrug: converted to dexamfetamine in red blood cells, not by liver CYP enzymes | Liver-enzyme variants matter less for the activation step | From the product information (HOLD) |
| **Dexamfetamine** | Part-cleared by the liver; urinary acidity changes how fast it is excreted (Tier A) | No routine genetic testing | (HOLD) |

**Interactions count as much as genes.** Some medicines that block CYP2D6 can raise atomoxetine levels, which is why prescribers check the full medication list. (Standard pharmacology; check the product information for specifics.)

## The deeper layer

- **Why the same dose acts differently.** Metabolism is one factor among several. Others include body weight, food, urinary pH, other drugs, receptor changes, and tolerance built up over time.
- **What genetics cannot yet do.** It cannot reliably say which stimulant will suit someone, how long a course should last, or whether it is safe to stop. Those remain clinical decisions based on observed response, and on what happens when the medicine is withdrawn.
- **Why this matters for continuity of care.** Because no genetic test identifies who can safely manage without treatment, "the test did not show you need it" is not a valid reason to stop medication in someone with clear symptoms and a documented response. The evidence of response is the clinical record.

## If someone cites genetics as a reason for a medication decision

Ask, in writing if possible:

1. Which gene or test are you relying on?
2. Which guideline or product information does it come from?
3. What threshold or result changes your decision?
4. Was the test actually done on me, or is this a general statement?
5. What does the guideline say to do in my situation?

If there is no test, no guideline and no result, the decision was made on other grounds and should be recorded as such.
