# Chen, Chad & Wheal (1996): what paired-pulse failures tell us about synaptic release

MJB ADHD Evidence Library · Evidence note · 16 November 2026  
Search identifiers: PMID 8945764 · DOI 10.1016/S0304-3940(96)13149-9

Chen and colleagues asked whether a second electrical stimulus was more likely to produce a detectable synaptic response when the first stimulus had already produced one. Their rat hippocampal recordings showed fewer second-pulse failures after a detectable first response. One recording gives all four paired counts, so readers can reproduce that comparison directly.

## What was measured

The researchers recorded excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) from CA1 pyramidal cells in adult rat hippocampal slices. They used minimal stimulation of afferents in the stratum radiatum, delivered two stimuli 85 ms apart and repeated each pair every two seconds. They selected 12 datasets with low recording noise, detectable failures and sufficient trials. Dataset sizes ranged from 202 to 685 pairs. They classified weak signals using the separation between noise and EPSCs in the amplitude distribution, typically around 1.5–2 times the noise standard deviation.

An observed failure in this analysis means the researchers detected no postsynaptic current above their threshold. They did not directly record whether an action potential arrived at the presynaptic terminal on each trial. The paper discusses the possibility that changes in excitability could recruit different subsets of axons between stimuli.

## The numbers readers can check

| First response | Second failure | Second success | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Observed failure | 50 | 116 | 166 |
| Detected EPSC | 57 | 228 | 285 |
| Total | 107 | 344 | 451 |

The table transcribes the counts from Chen et al., p. 206, Table 1. The second-pulse failure rate was 50/166 = **30.1%** after a first failure and 57/285 = **20.0%** after a first success. The overall first-pulse failure rate was 166/451 = **36.8%** in this example. [Download the four counts as CSV](chen-chad-wheal-1996-table-1.csv).

Table 2 reports the following mean failure rates across the 12 datasets.

| Comparison | Reported mean ± SEM |
| --- | ---: |
| First pulse, all trials | 36.0% ± 9.0 percentage points |
| Second pulse, all trials | 27.3% ± 7.7 percentage points |
| Second pulse after an observed first failure | 32.8% ± 8.2 percentage points |
| Second pulse after a detected first EPSC | 23.0% ± 6.3 percentage points |

The authors reported a reduction in second-pulse failures following a detected first EPSC (paired test, p < 0.005 versus overall first-pulse failure), and the comparison following an observed first failure did not reach significance. They also found no significant difference in the mean size of detectable EPSCs across the corresponding first- and second-pulse groups. Together, those observations support the interpretation that the increase in average response primarily reflected fewer failures in this preparation.

## What the data can support

The four counts support a likelihood for the joint observed outcomes in the one example recording. Figure 2 shows rates for individual datasets, and Table 2 gives summary statistics. The paper does not give exact four-cell counts and denominators for the other recordings. Treating the 451 trials as the full study or multiplying a reported group percentage by 451 would give an incorrect dataset.

The experiment used one pulse spacing. It cannot identify a time constant by itself. A common underlying release state could make successive responses correlate, and a missed axon recruitment can resemble an observed release failure. Direct recording of spike arrival, several pulse spacings and independent measurements of state would help distinguish these explanations. The nonsignificant comparison after a first failure leaves the size of any effect uncertain.

Debanne et al. provide a complementary endpoint. Figure 8 uses an amplitude ratio after an observed first failure, measured across several spacings in rat hippocampal slice cultures. Response probability and average current amplitude should be modelled as different observations when comparing the studies. Neither preparation directly tests ADHD or a human catecholamine mechanism.

## Where the citation goes next

Gengler, Hamilton & Hölscher (2010) explicitly cite Chen as reference 11 in their APPPS1-21 mouse study of hippocampal plasticity. Their paired-pulse experiment uses a different preparation and readout. It adds a separate dataset about short-term plasticity and does not reproduce Chen's conditional four-outcome analysis.
