# If I explained ADHD to an alien, I would start with the brain—not a questionnaire

*By Matthew J Bayliss, founder of the patient-led MJB ADHD research and advocacy resource*

Dr David Turner is right about one thing: general practice should not be expected to absorb specialist ADHD prescribing without the time, training, remuneration or clinical backup needed to do it safely. But his 2 November Pulse column undermines that legitimate argument with an account of ADHD that is neither a fair explanation of the condition nor an accurate description of the diagnostic standard.

His suggestion that ADHD can be diagnosed easily and quickly by questionnaire is not a mere difference of opinion. **It is directly at odds with NICE guidance.** Before we decide which clinicians should manage ADHD, surely we should agree on what competent ADHD care actually involves.

## If an alien asked me what ADHD is

I'd start with an analogy. Imagine that your brain is an air-traffic-control system. It is continually deciding which incoming information matters, which task deserves priority, when to initiate an action, when to inhibit a competing one and how to sustain effort when the immediate reward is small. The aircraft may have perfectly capable engines; the difficulty can lie in coordinating departures, arrivals and competing instructions.

In ADHD, these functions can be persistently and substantially unreliable. A person may understand exactly what needs doing, have the ability to do it, even care desperately about doing it—and still struggle to initiate, sequence, inhibit or sustain the actions involved. Context, interest, sleep, stress and urgency can change the observable difficulty. That variability does not by itself mean the underlying neurodevelopmental condition is imaginary.

This is a metaphor, not a claim that ADHD is one broken brain component. ADHD is heterogeneous. It has a substantial genetic contribution, with large studies identifying numerous associated genetic loci, and research implicates distributed neural networks influenced partly by dopamine and noradrenaline. No single neurotransmitter level, gene, brain scan or questionnaire is a clinical diagnostic test for ADHD.

Adverse housing, poor relationships, anxiety and depression can create serious distress and concentration difficulties. They can coexist with ADHD, intensify difficulties, or provide alternative explanations requiring assessment. They are not a basis for replacing a neurodevelopmental explanation with an assumption about parenting or dissatisfaction with life.

## A questionnaire is not a diagnosis

NICE NG87 requires a suitably qualified specialist to conduct a full clinical and psychosocial assessment, take a developmental and psychiatric history, gather appropriate observer information, assess impairment across settings, and evaluate coexisting conditions and alternative explanations. It specifically states that rating scales alone must not determine the diagnosis.

If a provider is diagnosing everyone who completes a screening form, that merits investigation. It is not an argument for treating screening forms as the legitimate diagnostic method, nor evidence that every private provider has a 100% diagnostic rate. That last claim requires transparent denominators, referral characteristics, assessment methods and independently verified outcomes.

There is a genuine debate about underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis. The government's October 2026 independent review warns that diagnostic quality and changing risks require attention. But it explicitly says the available evidence cannot establish overdiagnosis at scale. A 2026 British Journal of Psychiatry analysis likewise argues that the available UK evidence does not support a general claim of widespread overdiagnosis. An assertion of 'middle ground' is not a substitute for measuring the ground.

## Medicines are treatments, not diagnostic shortcuts

ADHD medication is not synonymous with 'amphetamines'. Options include methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine, dexamfetamine and non-stimulants such as atomoxetine, with choices depending on age, symptoms, tolerability, coexisting conditions, cardiovascular considerations and individual response.

Randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews show meaningful average benefits from several medicines. They also show side effects, variability between patients and important limits in long-term trial evidence. Fast symptom improvement after a stimulant does not confirm ADHD, just as an incomplete response does not invalidate a properly established diagnosis.

The clinical job is to assess, prescribe appropriately, monitor benefit and risk, and review what actually changes in a person's life—not to reduce a complex condition to 'give amphetamines, feel better'.

## GPs can refuse shared care. Patients must not be left without care.

This is where I agree with Dr Turner about an unfunded and inadequately supported transfer of specialist work. GPs cannot be forced into a shared-care agreement they lack the competence or capacity to deliver safely. Their concerns about training, monitoring, staffing and cost should be heard.

But there is a distinction between declining a proposed prescribing responsibility and letting the patient disappear between services. NICE says healthcare providers should ensure continuity of ADHD care. GMC guidance says that decisions about responsibility for continuing treatment should rest on the patient's interests, not convenience or cost, and that clinicians must communicate and arrange safe onward care. Government answers to Parliament confirm that, where shared care cannot be agreed after treatment has started, ongoing prescribing responsibility remains with the specialist clinician.

That does not mean a GP must issue a prescription without a safe clinical basis. It means the system must identify **who does retain responsibility**, what happens while a transfer is unresolved, and how foreseeable deterioration is assessed rather than administratively displaced. Primary care still has a vital role in recognising clinical risk, referrals, continuity information, coexisting illness, physical monitoring and patient advocacy. 'It isn't our prescription' cannot answer every question about an unwell person in front of you.

The NHS England ADHD Taskforce itself described a move toward a better-supported generalist model, while acknowledging lack of training, time, remuneration and specialist access. That is a case for building a workable service, not one for using patients as the pressure valve in a dispute between budgets and organisations.

## The patient is the part missing from the argument

I write from lived experience as well as reviewing the evidence. After years of established specialist ADHD treatment, I experienced a prolonged interruption and major deterioration in everyday function. I eventually arranged a private route to care myself. I have since documented the partial return of ordinary activities while continuing to investigate complex neurological symptoms. That is my individual account, not a controlled demonstration of medication causality.

What it illustrates is a basic question: when the specialist service, the GP and the commissioner each describe a process they have followed, **who verifies that the patient still has an effective treatment plan, a named decision-maker and somewhere safe to go when their condition deteriorates?**

If I had to explain our health system to the same alien, I would struggle more with that question than with explaining ADHD.

GPs deserve a properly resourced role. Patients deserve accurate science, competent assessment and something more fundamental than another referral: **a continuous line of clinical responsibility until the next clinician has actually accepted it**.
