# When Knowing Is Not Enough
## Working memory, retrieval and why diagrams change what the brain has to do

**MJB ADHD — evidence-graded downloadable explanation — editorial review copy, 20 October 2026**

**The central distinction:** Knowing something, retrieving it, holding its parts together, and translating it into words or action are different operations. External diagrams can handle part of the sequencing and search work that would otherwise fall on the reader.

**Evidence key:** This resource combines general cognitive science, observational/meta-analytic ADHD findings and explicitly labelled functional models. Group effects do not identify a specific individual's neural mechanism; technical computing metaphors are analogies, not literal descriptions of brain storage.

## The problem is not always missing knowledge

A person can **understand a concept**, remember its overall architecture and explain its importance, yet struggle to reproduce the precise words or sequence needed to communicate it. The visible result may look like forgetfulness or poor preparation. The underlying difficulty could instead involve access, temporary maintenance, selection, initiation or communication.

The common shorthand **“difficulty concentrating”** does not describe these stages with enough precision. To understand the functional cost, separate **what is known**, **what can be retrieved**, **what can be kept active**, and **what can be turned into action**.

Computer-system analogies and process maps are useful here by design. They are explanations of possible functions, **not claims that human memory literally operates like a hard drive, RAM chip or page file**. The brain is distributed, adaptive and state-dependent.

## Five different stages that can break down

1. **Representation.** Information, skills and conceptual relationships may exist in long-term memory. Neural representations are distributed and are not static files in one anatomical folder.
2. **Access.** A cue must activate relevant information. Retrieval depends on context and competition among associated items. Failure to retrieve does not prove an item was never learned.
3. **Active maintenance.** Working memory temporarily holds parts of an explanation during comparison, restructuring or hypothesis testing. Interruptions and competing demands can displace elements of the current task.
4. **Lexical selection and sequencing.** A concept may be recognised before the precise word is available. Selecting among near-synonyms while preserving the order of explanation adds selection and monitoring demands.
5. **Output and initiation.** Even after an explanation or plan is ready, beginning, sustaining and completing an outward action is a separate requirement. A good mental model is not identical to an executed task.

These stages interact and overlap rather than operating as a rigid assembly line. Figure 1 maps functional difficulties rather than localising neurological damage.

### Figure 1 — A concept can survive while its output pipeline fails

Accessible figure specification for the companion PDF (figure artwork generated with the review document):

`Knowledge/model → cue and access → working workspace → lexical selection → communication`

Alongside these nodes: `weak access` (cannot cue the right item), `insufficient holding` (the preceding element drops away), `lexical competition` (a near-correct word appears), and `output sequencing` (clear concept, incoherent delivery). **No arrow is intended to assert anatomical or exclusive causation.**

## Why ADHD makes this more than an analogy

These cognitive limitations occur in people generally. However, ADHD is associated with measurable **group-level working-memory and executive-function differences**. A meta-analysis of 38 adult studies reported moderate differences across verbal and visuospatial working-memory domains [1]. An earlier meta-analysis of 83 studies identified medium-sized differences across executive-function tasks, while emphasising that no single deficit is universal or sufficient to explain all ADHD [2].

Word retrieval requires a separate qualification. A **2026 meta-analysis of 16 cross-sectional studies**, including 1,167 people (566 with ADHD), reported group differences in timed **phonemic and semantic verbal fluency** (Hedges g = −0.44 and −0.51) [3]. Those tests measure word generation under constrained conditions; they are not a direct measurement of every real-world instance in which a person recognises a concept but cannot retrieve the desired word.

The supported claim is a **higher probability of measurable difficulty in relevant operations**, not that everyone with ADHD has the same deficit. Sleep, pain, context, medication, development, comorbidities and individual strengths can all affect performance. Fluent expression on one occasion is not evidence that the same fluency will always be available.

## External structure is a cognitive tool

Researchers use the term **cognitive offloading** for actions or tools that change memory and processing requirements: reminders, notes, calendars and diagrams [4]. Offloading is not restricted to ADHD. It is an ordinary adaptation to the limits and costs of unaided cognition.

A foundational analysis by Larkin and Simon (1987) showed that some diagrammatic representations make **relations spatially explicit**, avoiding mental searches and computations needed to extract the same information from sequential descriptions [5]. Diagrams are not always superior: confusing arrows, unnecessary colour and overcrowding can increase work.

A 2024 computational study modelled the choice between internally maintaining information and setting external reminders as a trade-off between memory's opportunity cost and the modest setup cost of offloading [6]. **This is a computational account of reminder behaviour, not a clinical trial proving that flowcharts treat ADHD.**

The practical point is that **a process map can perform part of the sequencing work for the reader**. They can locate the next node, trace an influence, and return after interruption without reconstructing the whole explanation.

### Figure 2 — Same information, different workload

**Text-only route:** remember starting point; hold simultaneous relationships; mentally assemble order; search for the preceding clause; reconstruct after interruption.

**External process map:** see named input; trace a visible relation; identify the next node by position; resume at the preserved location.

The two displays may contain the same facts, but they do not impose identical operations on the reader. This is a general information-processing proposition; the clinical benefits of a specific layout still need evaluation.

## A useful diagram must carry the right information

A good process map makes **components, relationships, direction, timing, gates, failure points and observed outputs** visible. The reader should be able to answer: *Where am I? What happens next? What must I hold in mind?*

A bad map may hide alternate explanations or imply proof through arrows. Biological diagrams should distinguish **established**, **conditional**, **hypothesised** and **unknown** links. A process dictionary can store this extra detail without forcing it all onto the overview at once.

External tools have costs too. Somebody must set the reminder and notice it. Broken links, inaccessible documents and outdated instructions can create new failure points. Experiments have documented cases where external storage changes internal recall and increases certain false-recall responses [7]. This is not a reason to abandon external aids; it is a reason to make them **reliable, accessible, transparent and resumable**.

## Effort is not the same as completion

Executive-function difficulty can require considerable preparation, repeated attempts and context reconstruction before a task begins. The person may experience effort and fatigue while the visible output remains zero.

This is a **functional description and resource-cost hypothesis**, not a validated claim that every abandoned task expends more ATP or energy than its successful completion. Subjective effort, elapsed time, number of attempts and physiological expenditure are distinct measures.

### Figure 3 — Why zero completion need not mean zero effort

`Intention → start attempts → interference → opportunity window closes`

**Result:** no completed output; non-zero time, effort and recovery cost.

An assessment that observes only completed actions will overlook the cost of repeated unsuccessful starts. Relevant variables include **initiation delay, lost intentions, time to recover context, compensatory effort, interruption tolerance and the opportunity cost of completing one task rather than another**.

## Medication, skills and support are different interventions

Medication can change performance without giving somebody new knowledge. A **2024 meta-analysis of longer-term trials** reported that methylphenidate improved several attention, inhibition, working-memory and reaction-time endpoints versus placebo, with small-to-medium group effects [8]. Atomoxetine improved several domains but not working memory in that pooled analysis. Neither finding quantifies the effects of dexamfetamine on any individual person's lexical retrieval, and neither proves that one molecular mechanism explains all cognitive performance.

Environmental support and skills-based therapy are different interventions. In a randomised study of **88 adults**, structured metacognitive therapy targeting time management, organisation and planning showed advantages against supportive psychotherapy [9]. This does not prove medication can be withdrawn once strategies are learned, nor that someone will always be able to deploy those strategies unaided.

**NICE NG87** recommends written/picture-based medication instructions and **visual reminders**. It explicitly asks clinicians to take account of the time and organisational skills required to engage in non-pharmacological treatments, and suggests continued support to maintain learned strategies [10]. A useful accommodation is not evidence of laziness and success with it is not, in itself, evidence it is unnecessary.

## Design specifications for a low-burden ADHD resource

- **Keep the active step visible.** Highlight the current process node; save location so someone can stop and resume.
- **Make language operational.** Prefer named actions and conditions to advice like “focus” or “try harder.”
- **Show uncertainty.** Distinguish measured edges from plausible branches, contradictions and untested assumptions.
- **Offer diagrams AND heading-based accessible prose.** Include full alt-description, useful contrast and do not encode status using colour alone.
- **Minimise setup cost.** Templates, labelled decisions, reusable flow charts and persistent context should reduce the need to invent a new management system.
- **Measure sustainable outcomes.** Evaluate interruption recovery, repeatability, completion, measured accuracy and perceived resource cost alongside task speed.

A good aid does not require the person to memorise how to use it while they are experiencing the difficulty it is intended to address.

## What is established, and what remains hypothetical?

**Established at group/research level:** ADHD-related differences in working memory and some executive function domains; improvements on selected cognitive outcomes in trials of certain medications; information-processing advantages of some well-designed external representations [1, 2, 4, 5, 8].

**Context-specific evidence:** ADHD verbal-fluency group differences, guideline-supported visual reminders and certain structured ADHD therapies [3, 9, 10].

**Explanatory and testable hypotheses:** a specific individual may retain conceptual knowledge while experiencing difficulty in lexical selection, working-space maintenance or goal persistence; external diagrams may release sufficient capacity to improve complex explanation or return-to-task performance.

**Not established:** a literal faulty RAM or voltage deficit in ADHD; the inference that every word-finding difficulty has the same cause; that visual diagrams cure ADHD; that medication-specific subjective clarity identifies a unique molecular mechanism.

**Proposed discriminating experiment:** Randomise or counterbalance adults with ADHD to **information-equivalent** paragraph and interactive-flow-map formats under different working-memory/interruption demands. Measure answer accuracy, explanation coherence, resumption time, subjective effort and willingness to re-use the tool; include controls for reading ability, diagrams, baseline working memory, visual preferences and task familiarity. Successful results would support specific interface benefits, not a universal neurochemical explanation.
