# Can the body learn to respond to a medicine before the medicine acts?

**Working editorial conclusion:** Yes, conditioned drug-associated cues can generate measurable physiological changes, sometimes including motor or immune responses. The evidence does NOT establish that the body universally recognises a drug's precise molecular 'signature' before exposure. Distinguish the molecule's receptor pharmacology, known treatment ritual and predictive learned signals.

## The causal categories to keep separate

1. **Direct pharmacology:** active drug exposure, receptor occupancy, ion-channel and cellular effects.
2. **Cephalic/anticipatory responses:** environmental sights, smells, taste and learned food cues can trigger autonomic and endocrine changes in minutes, even before meaningful nutrient absorption. Evidence for functional therapeutic benefit in specific diseases is more limited.
3. **Learned drug conditioning:** pairing a cue (pill appearance, flavour, context) with prior pharmacological effects can train later cue-evoked biological responses.
4. **Verbal expectancy and nocebo:** what a person is told about treatment may change subjective and sometimes objective responses, including adverse effects. Information is part of the intervention context, not molecular proof.
5. **Outcomes and confounds:** distinguish neural activity, muscle rigidity, cytokines, pain ratings, blood drug levels, physiological parameters and diary report. Regression-to-mean, natural fluctuation and reporting bias are not the same as a biological placebo response.

## Four primary experimental anchors

**Learned motor response in Parkinson disease (2016):** Placebo given after two to four previous apomorphine treatments produced altered thalamic neuron activity and reduced wrist rigidity; first-ever placebo without conditioning did not. The size/duration of conditioned response related to number of pharmacological pairings. Strength: direct human single-neuron and clinical motor outcome. Limit: specialised Parkinson patients, invasive recording and prior apomorphine exposure; not general ADHD drug 'recognition'. [Benedetti and colleagues, *J Physiol* 2016; PMID 26861164](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26861164/).

**Learned immune response (2002):** Cyclosporine A with a novel flavoured drink across acquisition sessions; after washout, flavoured drink plus placebo evoked measurable suppression of IL-2/IFN-gamma measures and lymphocyte proliferation. Strength: measurable human immunological endpoints, double-blind/placebo control. Limit: trained stimulus, immunosuppressive indication, not general drug response. [Goebel and colleagues, *FASEB J* 2002; PMID 12468450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12468450/).

**Expectations with remifentanil (2011):** At fixed drug concentration and standardized heat pain, positive and negative analgesic expectancy had strong effects on pain reports and fMRI pain-related activity. Authors described increased analgesia with positive expectancy and loss of subjective analgesia with negative expectancy. **Important caveat:** this study did not independently vary drug presence and expectancy. [Bingel and colleagues, *Sci Transl Med* 2011; PMID 21325618](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21325618/).

**Factorial counter-evidence (2012):** Separately manipulated active remifentanil and expectancy using balanced placebo/open-hidden designs. Observed additive/dissociable pain and brain responses, **not** a demonstrated pharmacological potentiation interaction. Strength: study disentangles expectancy and drug factors; cautions against treating the 2011 study as proof that belief doubled intrinsic drug potency. [Atlas and colleagues, *J Neurosci* 2012; PMID 22674280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22674280/).

**Open-label placebo (2010):** 80 people with irritable bowel syndrome randomised to openly identified placebo plus explanation or no-treatment matched clinician interaction. The openly labelled placebo group improved more on symptom-report scales. Cautions: small, subjective condition; expectation-supporting explanation was part of intervention; does not establish cure or substitute for disease-specific medicine. [Kaptchuk and colleagues, *PLoS One* 2010; PMID 21203519](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21203519/).

## Why this could affect clinical trials

A conventional drug-vs-placebo randomised blinded trial balances many contextual effects between groups and estimates the *incremental effect of active assignment relative to control*. It does not simply exclude the brain's anticipatory and learned response: both arms can have those responses, and the result is interpreted conditionally. An open-label trial can differ in expectancy from blinded conditions, but honest explanation, consent and therapeutic alliance still matter. A **balanced placebo design** (actual drug vs placebo crossed with told drug vs told placebo) specifically identifies drug, expectation and interaction terms; deceptive designs require strong ethics. A historical conditioning factor should be measured rather than assumed equal.

Crucially, placebo does not mean 'imaginary' and does not make a reported effect pharmacologically diagnostic. A cue-evoked reduction in motor rigidity does not replace dopamine agonism in treatment, and conditioned immune effects do not establish safe medication withdrawal.

## Lactose and 'inert' excipients: separate hypothesis

**Biochemistry:** Intestinal lactase cleaves milk lactose to glucose and galactose. The body can sense gut glucose and galactose via intestinal/vagal pathways in *mice*, but those results do not show that ordinary lactose intake rapidly repairs neuronal calcium handling or improves neurological symptoms. Source: [Tan and colleagues, *Nature* 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2199-7), animal gut-brain physiology.

**Dairy vs starch:** Philadelphia Original lists about 4.3g sugars per 100g soft cheese, whereas Ritz Original lists 61g carbohydrate per 100g, largely derived from wheat flour, not lactose. Food portions and actual current label matter. A cheese-and-cracker meal cannot isolate lactose from fats, starch, proteins or learned food cues. Sources: [Philadelphia UK](https://www.philadelphia.eu/uk/products/philadelphia-original/index.html), [Ritz Original](https://www.tesco.com/shop/en-GB/products/316558663).

**Tablet excipients:** Lactose is frequently used in drug tablets. In a controlled crossover study of 77 lactose maldigesters, 400mg lactose produced no significant difference from placebo on measured hydrogen expiration or gastrointestinal symptoms; this cannot rule out every individual sensitivity or every formulation effect. Source: [Montalto and colleagues, 2008, PMID 18657134](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18657134/). Distinguish lactose intolerance (GI mechanisms) from the effects of active medicine and expectancy. Other excipients sometimes merit specific assessment but generic 'inactive' cannot automatically be equated to biologically inert under all conditions. [Reker and colleagues, *Sci Transl Med* 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7122736/).

## Mechanism-level discriminators for research

- What did the organism learn: molecular pharmacology, the taste/appearance, a remembered consequence or an explicit verbal promise? Only the cue and pharmacological pairing can be directly separated in controlled experiments.
- Does the response occur before plausible absorption? Verify time course against measured drug or nutrient concentration rather than subjective onset alone.
- Which domain changes first: objective rigidity/EMG, autonomic measures, pain intensity, perceived salience, or task performance?
- Is expectancy an **additive** process or does it **interact** with drug exposure? Test both in factorial design; preserve contrary data.
- Does longer or more diverse prior exposure train the same or different response? Avoid assuming a familiar medicine's learned effect is universal.
- For gastrointestinal nutrient effects, compare lactose with lactose-free same-macronutrient food only in ethically approved studies; patients should not deliberately provoke concerning neurological symptoms.
