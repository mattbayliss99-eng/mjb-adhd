# Lactose, dairy and calcium: seven mechanisms, seven distinct compartments

**Editorial disposition:** retain as an evidence audit and possible expandable section within existing compounds/calcium physiology pages. No medical intervention, public site change, diagnosis, private history or patient-specific inference authorised. Evidence investigated 19 October 2026. In particular, a rapid symptom response following dairy would not establish direction of serum calcium change, altered neuronal calcium clearance, membrane repair or a lactose-specific effect.

## Ground rule — do not collapse the compartments

1. **Food lumen:** calcium can be free/soluble, complexed, or within milk casein/calcium phosphate assemblies.
2. **Intestinal cell:** sodium–glucose cotransport, water movement, apical TRPV6/Cav1.3 channels, intracellular calcium buffers, PMCA1b/NCX1 basolateral efflux, CaSR regulation.
3. **Blood/systemic endocrine loop:** ionized vs total calcium, phosphate, kidney/bone, PTH, active vitamin D and FGF23. Diet may change absorption without meaningfully altering plasma ionized calcium.
4. **Neuronal/cellular cytosol:** cytosolic Ca2+ transients, sodium membrane gradient, ATP-dependent pumps.
5. **ER/SR:** SERCA pumps; IP3R/RyR release; STIM-ORAI refill.
6. **Lysosomes:** TRPML1 and related release, ER–lysosome and mitochondria–lysosome contact sites; disputed refill machinery.
7. **Mineral deposits:** casein micelle calcium phosphate, extracellular matrix-vesicle mineralization, tissue calcification and synaptic vesicles are different entities.

**Classification legend:** A = human direct intervention for specified endpoint; B = experimental animal/ex-vivo cell mechanism; C = biophysical/structural established chemistry or indirect relevance; H = untested link or speculative patient extrapolation. An established cellular mechanism is NOT an established food/clinical treatment response.

## Candidate 1. Calcium solubility and intestinal bioavailability

**Mechanistic model:** Luminal mineral chemistry and casein phosphopeptides influence soluble versus colloidal calcium; intestinal absorption proceeds through transcellular and paracellular routes. Lactose is normally split by lactase into glucose + galactose, which may alter sodium/water absorption and thereby calcium flux. Absorbed calcium is **greater entry into systemic circulation**, not proof of cellular "clearance."

**Human positive trial:** Cochet et al. 1983 [P1], dual-isotope experiment, 12 subjects with normal lactase and seven with lactase deficiency, 50 g lactose versus none. Fractional calcium absorption rose from 0.224±0.039 to 0.356±0.076 in lactase-sufficient subjects but decreased from 0.255±0.039 to 0.209±0.032 in deficient subjects. Small samples, large lactose challenge, acute kinetic endpoint.

**Human physiologic refinement:** Griessen et al. 1989 [P2], dual isotopes in small groups (n=9 glucose, n=8 galactose, n=20 lactitol) observed ~30% increase in fractional calcium uptake with absorbable sugars and 15% decrease with poorly absorbed lactitol. Schuette et al. 1989 [P3], n=10, 30cm jejunal perfusion; net sodium/water transport associated with calcium uptake, but only one pairwise net-Ca comparison was significant. Thus association and causal components are not identical.

**Counterevidence:** 2000 randomised crossover n=11 measured strontium tracer absorption (proxy, not direct calcium) and found no increase with 35g lactose or glucose+galactose [P4]. 1986 n=20 study comparing hydrolysed/unhydrolysed milk also requires examining full endpoint details [P5]. 2012 review finds human lactose effect inconsistent [R1].

**Dairy matrix distinction:** Milk's casein micelles sequester amorphous calcium phosphate [B1]. Adding casein phosphopeptides (CPP) to milk did not significantly increase calcium absorption in a 15-person randomised isotope study [P6]. A second 15-person crossover study reported more absolute calcium absorbed in CPP-supplemented drinks but CPP drinks also delivered additional calcium; fractional absorption decreased modestly and authors found no biologically useful effect [P7]. Therefore **casein calcium binding** is real chemistry but **CPP supplementation to improve calcium status** is not established.

**Inference limit:** No studies identified that show lactose-containing cheese rapidly removes accumulated calcium from brain tissue or reverses acute neurological rigidity. Cheese/cream cheese and milk differ in lactose, calcium, phosphate, fat and proteins; brand and portions matter.

## Candidate 2. Calcium-sensing receptors (CaSR)

**Established:** CaSR is a G-protein-coupled extracellular calcium sensor in parathyroid and kidney controlling endocrine/blood-calcium homeostasis. Expression and functional regulation in intestinal epithelium also exist. In mouse intestine ex vivo, acute basolateral CaSR activation by calcium or cinacalcet reduced net calcium absorption, dependent on functional TRPV6; chronic stimulation reduced calcium transport gene expression [B2]. Separate mouse/intestinal studies found a CaSR–PLC–Ca2+–intermediate-conductance K+ pathway affecting dipeptide absorption [B3].

**Discriminator:** Calcium occupancy versus lactase/lactose: does the experimental preparation change luminal calcium independently of sugar, and is CaSR activated at the actual surface being exposed? **No direct primary evidence found that ordinary lactose uniquely agonises CaSR**. Local CaSR intestinal effects cannot be read as neuronal CaSR modulation, and do not demonstrate rapid symptom relief.

## Candidate 3. Phosphate balance and dairy calcium–phosphate chemistry

**Dairy chemistry:** Milk caseins sequester calcium phosphate nanoclusters; soluble/colloidal phosphate proportions and pH matter [B1].

**Systemic:** Intestine/kidney/bone regulate phosphate via PTH, vitamin D and FGF23. A 2024 two-trial randomised crossover report compared high phosphate-additive vs otherwise different meals at similar reported phosphorus amounts (562–572 mg; Ca:P molar ratio 0.26 vs 0.48), plus a 5-day phosphate-diet manipulation. Serum phosphate, urinary phosphate and calcium handling changed; meals differed in more than one component and do not represent a pure dairy comparison [P8]. An eight-person acute phosphate-meal crossover found phosphate changed within an hour whereas FGF23 effects emerged later and at high loads [P9]. Not every phosphate challenge decreases circulating ionised calcium: a separate 18-person oral loading experiment found no significant ionised Ca/PTH change over five hours [P10].

**Inference limit:** Calcium phosphate complexation in milk or intestinal contents is **not** the same as rapid dissolution of intracellular neuronal calcium; further, adding phosphorus does not selectively bind excess intracellular calcium in a safe therapeutic manner. Hypothesised lactose→phosphate→faster brain calcium clearance has no validated causal chain.

## Candidate 4. Calcium buffering and clearance

**Established:** Calcium signals depend on location, free Ca2+ levels, buffering (calbindin, calmodulin and other binding proteins), extrusion via PMCA (ATP-dependent) and NCX (Na+ gradient-dependent), and sequestration in ER/mitochondria/lysosomes. In the intestine, calbindin-D9k and PMCA1b are part of nutrient calcium transport [R2]. In neurons, plasma membrane NCX is bidirectional; depolarization or increased intracellular Na+ can favour reverse-mode Ca2+ entry [P11]. Experiments with excitotoxic glutamate in cultured rat hippocampal neurons implicated NMDAR and reverse NCX in delayed Ca2+ dysregulation [P12]. This is a cell stress model, not evidence of a particular human condition.

**Inference limit:** A sodium-dependent transporter's capacity and a food's effect on intestinal sodium absorption do not automatically imply a neuronal sodium gradient defect. Neither dairy lipid nor lactose is shown to accelerate neural PMCA or SERCA activity in vivo acutely.

## Candidate 5. Endoplasmic-reticulum calcium storage

**Established:** ER/SR calcium entry through SERCA ATPase, release via IP3Rs/RyRs; depleted ER triggers STIM1-ORAI1 store-operated entry in appropriate cell types. A human HeLa cell study showed ER calcium stores could refill in 2min through microdomain coupling even after substantial STIM1 knockdown, illustrating why gross cytosolic calcium signals may not reveal store refilling [P13].

**Relevant intestinal crossover:** 2021 experiment in mouse jejunal epithelium linked glucose/SGLT1 Na+ flux, apical Cav1.3 calcium influx, RyR-mediated ER calcium release, STIM1/Orai1 and NCX1 [P14]. **Contradiction:** 2015 in vivo mouse research found that luminal glucose did not enhance active calcium absorption (P=.6) or support the proposed critical Cav1.3 role [P15]. Tissue preparations, flux directions, glucose concentrations and measured outcomes differ. Do not sum these experiments as independent confirmation of one complete pathway.

**Inference limit:** Real calcium stores and entero-epithelial signalling do not show that a lactose-containing meal corrects neuronal ER calcium release or muscle relaxation.

## Candidate 6. Lysosomal calcium handling

**Established:** Lysosomes are calcium stores, with TRPML1-mediated release regulating trafficking/fusion. 2016 HEK293 and fibroblast assays indicated ER Ca2+/IP3R-dependent lysosome refill, not directly dependent on V-ATPase acidification in their experimental conditions [P16]. 2019 ER-lysosome microscopy/local calcium sensors further supported an ER-to-lysosome delivery mechanism [P17]. 2020 microscopy showed TRPML1 Ca2+ release locally transferred to mitochondria via VDAC1/MCU contact sites, including dysfunctional dynamics in MLIV patient-derived fibroblasts [P18]. A 2024 cellular study reported acute lysosomal fusion/acidification changes 10–20min after experimental TRPML1 activation [P19].

**Unresolved:** Exact lysosomal calcium uptake transporter, relative importance of acidic pH gradient versus direct ER transfer, and roles of TPC1/2, ATP13A2, cell type and physiological state. A 2024 eLife calcium-inflammasome article attracted explicit reviewer concern that major causal claims had incomplete experimental support [R3]. These findings do not establish lactose/cheese as a lysosomal therapy.

## Candidate 7. Cellular ion gradients

**Strongest joint mechanistic intersection with lactose, confined to intestinal epithelium:**

Lactose → lactase → glucose/galactose → apical SGLT1 co-uptake with Na+ → basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase restores gradient → paracellular osmosis/water movement (possible calcium solvent drag) and voltage-dependent cross-talk with calcium transport. Rat jejunal electrophysiology showed glucose-dependent, nifedipine-sensitive 45Ca entry [P20]. Mouse intestinal epithelium 2021 showed complementary regulation involving Cav1.3, RyR, STIM/Orai and NCX1 [P14]. But 2015 in vivo mouse [P15] did not confirm that the Cav1.3 proposed increase in calcium absorption occurs robustly under tested conditions.

**Established inter-organelle ion feedback:** In cell models, Na+ changes and mitochondrial NCLX can affect ORAI1 store-operated calcium entry through mitochondrial redox [P21]. This is not evidence that intestinal sodium uptake immediately changes neuronal NCLX or brain calcium status.

**Key directional correction:** Increasing fractional intestinal Ca absorption is not a calcium removal process. In the kidney, calcium homeostasis uses filtering/reabsorption under PTH and CaSR control. Neuronal cytosolic calcium depends on local fast transport; extracellular ionized calcium is regulated separately. The same ion can be transported in opposite directions by NCX depending on membrane and ionic gradients. More calcium in the diet is not equivalent to higher cytosolic neuronal calcium.

## Evidence grading / decision matrix

| Candidate | Normal biological mechanism | Direct lactose/dairy-to-calcium evidence | Evidence for rapid neurological benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium solubility / uptake | A (human isotope studies) + C (milk matrix chemistry) | A but mixed and context-dependent | H: no direct evidence |
| CaSR | B (intestinal rodent/ex vivo) + established systemic physiology | H: no direct lactose/CaSR target evidence | H |
| Phosphate balance | A (human controlled phosphate feeding) + C (milk chemistry) | C only; no specific lactose causation | H |
| Buffering / clearance | B (cellular transporter experiments), established general physiology | H for increased neuronal clearance after dairy | H |
| ER stores | B (cell experiments; intestinal epithelial crosstalk) | H for lactose→neuronal ER | H |
| Lysosomal calcium | B (cell experiments, patient-derived fibroblasts) | H for dietary lactose→lysosomes | H |
| Cellular ion gradients | A (human jejunal sugar/Ca associations) + B (animal epithelial transporter work) | Strong plausible intestinal link; mechanism direction and relevance vary | H |

This table explicitly avoids granting clinical outcome credibility from mechanistic proximity or repeated reviews of the same fundamental experiments.

## Three experimental/clinical questions for future research

1. **Lactose itself versus hydrolysis products versus dairy matrix:** In controlled human tracer experiments, under comparable calcium load, do lactose, lactose-hydrolyzed dairy, lactose-free nutrient-matched controls, and fat-matched foods show different fractional Ca uptake? Record lactase status, Na/water handling, timing. Ethical diet studies do not imply unsupervised symptom provocation.
2. **Transport causality:** In parallel intestinal organoid models, manipulate SGLT1, Cav1.3, TRPV6, CaSR and NCX separately under physiological doses of luminal nutrients; measure direction, apical/serosal flux, electrical currents and changes in intracellular Ca. Crucially test whether 2015 mouse negative result replicates under 2007/2021 conditions.
3. **Compartment discrimination:** Determine which calcium compartment is actually perturbed before invoking ER or lysosomal mechanisms. Use clinically appropriate ionized Ca, PTH, renal function, phosphate/magnesium and actual nutrient/medication records where indicated; lab physiology cannot identify neuronal calcium store defects directly. For basic science, quantify organelle-specific reporters (ER and lysosomal and mitochondrial) and ATP/sodium gradients, not inferred from systemic serum Ca.

## 19 Oct 2026 primary-source correction: fabricated lactose → NCLX “flush” mechanism

An externally generated explanatory paragraph claimed: **oral lactose chelates calcium, acts as a selective sieve at cell membranes, modulates plasma-membrane calcium entry, activates mitochondrial NCLX, and rapidly flushes overloaded mitochondrial calcium**. The explanation attached several legitimate-looking scientific papers. Cross-checked below as a **source-link integrity case study**, not a viable therapeutic mechanism.

| Citation supplied | What the source actually discusses | Missing leap |
|---|---|---|
| Charley & Saltman 1963, DOI 10.1126/science.139.3560.1205, PMID 14020242 | In vitro lactose (among other sugars/polyols) delays calcium precipitation in bicarbonate and forms a labelled Ca–lactose complex; *possible intestinal absorption* implications | No cell membrane selectivity, mitochondrial transporter intervention, NCLX stimulation, or brain outcomes |
| Finkel et al. 2015, PMID 25999421, PMCID PMC6296495 | Review of MCU/NCLX and mitochondrial calcium entry/efflux and energy | No lactose-fed group, no effect of calcium-lactose complexes on NCLX |
| Ilesanmi-Oyelere & Kruger 2020, PMID 33072800, PMC7539038 | Review of milk constituents, lactose and intestinal calcium absorption/bone health | No claim of selective chelation-driven neuronal calcium discharge |
| Walkon et al. 2022, PMID 36551319, PMC9775684 | Review/perspective concerning experimental mitochondrial overload, calcium-phosphate buffering and respiratory capacity in models, including isolated mitochondria | No oral lactose treatment or demonstration of mitochondrial calcium reversal by dairy |
| Matuz-Mares et al. 2022, PMID 35624667, PMC9138001 | Review of mitochondrial calcium imbalance and diseases | Reviews are not experimental proof of lactose-evoked NCLX activation |

**Mechanism disaggregation:** (1) weak/moderate soluble Ca–lactose complex formation in an experimental solution; (2) intestinal lactose hydrolysis to glucose and galactose by lactase and sodium-coupled absorption via SGLT1; (3) nuanced calcium transport/buffering across cell and organelle membranes; (4) mitochondrial MCU influx and NCLX-dependent efflux controlled by local electrochemical gradients and signalling. These independently supported nodes do **not** supply experimentally verified causal arrows connecting dietary lactose to neuronal mitochondrial calcium clearance.

**Falsifiable bridging experiment, if scientifically motivated:** In a relevant human neural cellular system with controlled substrate exposure, quantify *intact lactose presence/transport*, mitochondrial free and total calcium, cytosolic sodium, membrane potential, NCLX-specific perturbation, and ATP before and after exposure; compare physiological glucose, galactose and vehicle controls. Even positive in vitro findings would not establish ordinary dietary lactose reaches brain neurons or treats symptoms. Ethics, transport feasibility, and physiological exposure limits are critical; no patient self-experiment.

**Clinical translation:** A reported reduction in eye/head pain or altered photophobia after a meal is a report of changing symptoms, not a direct measurement of calcium's spatial distribution, and should not become proof of a mitochondrial mechanism. The absence of a verified biochemical link should be preserved as a negative evidence result, not merely left vague.

## 19 October 2026 additional research: crystal arthritis and a real pyrophosphate paradox

**Evidence boundary:** Calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD, acute form historically 'pseudogout') is a recognised calcium-containing **extracellular crystal** disorder of cartilage/joints, different from urate gout. Basic calcium phosphate/hydroxyapatite crystals contribute to calcific tendinitis and other periarticular calcification. EULAR 2023 imaging guidance distinguishes monosodium urate, calcium pyrophosphate, and basic calcium phosphate: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103298/ . The 2023 validated ACR/EULAR CPPD research classification criteria distinguish imaging, synovial crystal identification and predisposing conditions: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10543651/ .

**Actual biochemical paradox:** Extracellular inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) ordinarily antagonises hydroxyapatite nucleation/mineralisation, but higher local PPi combined with calcium can favour CPP crystal deposition. ANKH transports ATP/citrate toward extracellular cartilage matrix; ENPP1 converts extracellular ATP to AMP + PPi; TNAP hydrolyses PPi to orthophosphate (Pi). A shift in PPi/Pi ratio can change the favored crystal chemistry. This is a local tissue biochemistry mechanism, NOT equivalent to higher blood calcium, high neuronal cytosolic calcium, or dairy-triggered CPPD. 2024 peer-reviewed review: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11377473/ ; 2026 mechanistic review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13356900/ (retain review classification, not independent direct proof of each causal edge). The exact roles and directionality of ANKH/ENPP1/TNAP across human disease subtypes remain debated.

**Mineral handling ≠ instant reversal:** CPPD is generally associated with age-related cartilage changes, haemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia and rare genetic disorders; a young person without objective joint swelling, crystals or imaging cannot be identified as having CPPD. Note calcium-hydroxyapatite deposition in tendon can cause especially painful resorptive episodes (clinical review), a secondary paradox: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21638-calcific-tendonitis . EULAR management notes no reliably established curative approach to dissolve CPP deposits in tissues: https://ard.bmj.com/content/70/4/571 . New hot, swollen joint requires clinical assessment to exclude septic arthritis (NICE NG219): https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng219/chapter/Recommendations .

**Chocolate composition and lactose: contradicts simple lactose-dose prediction.** Arla UK provides broad typical estimates (not a measured controlled brand comparison): white chocolate ~2–8g, milk ~4–7g, dark often ~0–1.3g lactose per 100g; white may contain more lactose than milk, and ranges overlap, so preference for milk over white cannot be evidence of a monotonic lactose dose-response. https://www.arlafoods.co.uk/brands/lactofree/all-articles/is-chocolate-lactose-free/ . Actual Cadbury Crunchie 40g uses sugar, glucose syrup, skimmed milk powder and whey permeate (and fats), and has 26g total sugars per bar, which are **not** synonymous with lactose: https://www.cadbury.co.uk/products/cadbury-crunchie-chocolate-bar-40g/ . Product formulations and brands vary. No warranted claim that chocolate, lactose or honeycomb reverses tissue crystal accumulation.

**Prodrug naming correction:** Lisdexamfetamine (Elvanse) is covalently conjugated dexamfetamine + *L-lysine* and is hydrolysed primarily by red blood cells, not lysosomes. Source official UK SmPC: https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/14091/smpc .

**Compartment precaution:** Intestinal luminal soluble calcium, serum ionised calcium, cartilage matrix calcium phosphate/pyrophosphate, neuronal cytosolic Ca2+ transients, mitochondria/ER stores and long-term pathologic crystal deposits are not interchangeable measures or disorders. Normal blood calcium is essential for life and tightly regulated; without measurement it cannot be classified as 'bad' compared with gut calcium. Preserve this as a source-checked mechanistic correction, not an inferred personal diagnosis or a recommendation to restrict calcium.


## Directional perturbations are not automatically inverse tests (19 October addendum)

**Experimental logic:** An apparent adverse response during a supplement exposure does not warrant deliberately lowering systemic calcium as the inverse condition. Calcium availability has separate compartments (diet, intestinal lumen, serum ionised Ca2+, extracellular nerve environment, cytosol, ER, lysosome, mitochondrial matrix). Increasing total oral salt mass need not proportionally raise blood calcium; low blood ionized calcium can itself cause increased neuromuscular excitability/spasm. Moreover, an ingredient weight is not an elemental calcium weight: CaCO3 ~40% Ca by mass vs calcium citrate ~21%. [NIH ODS calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/). UK NHS indicates high supplemental intake above 1500mg/day can harm; total adult upper tolerable intake 2500mg/day is US NIH limit for ages 19–50, including all intake sources [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/calcium/). **An inverse mechanistic study requires measurement of actual target perturbation and safety controls**, not an unsupervised opposite exposure. In clinical assessment, review product labels, renal function, PTH/phosphate, adjusted and/or ionised calcium and other tests as clinically indicated.

**Potential maladaptive feedback, not proven immune self-attack:** Acute protective pain amplification and persistent gain/hypersensitivity can be discussed through central sensitisation and nociceptive regulation. [Latremoliere & Woolf 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2750819/) is a well-developed mechanistic model. A [2024 opposing review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39278607/) notes a shortage of direct proof that central sensitisation is necessary/sufficient to explain particular chronic pain syndromes. The analogy of 'protection becoming harmful' is not clinical evidence for antibodies/autoimmune disease. Treat as competing models.

**Energetic positive-feedback candidate:** Excess mitochondrial Ca2+ buffering can impair OXPHOS; reduced ATP could theoretically impair PMCA/SERCA and secondary calcium clearance, producing a positive-feedback loop. [Walkon et al 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36551319/) discusses calcium load and mitochondrial energetic inhibition; [Rizzuto et al / neuronal review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3489481/) discusses bidirectionality. This coupling is a biologically plausible conditional mechanism, NOT evidence that an oral calcium supplement caused a human 'electron transport gridlock'. Moderate calcium also supports mitochondrial metabolism; direct measurement and cell-specific assays are essential.

**Do not conflate drug pathways:** Cocaine can cause frequency-dependent voltage-gated sodium-channel block, but also blocks dopamine/noradrenaline/serotonin transporters and carries substantial ischemic and arrhythmic risk. The subjective effect of a monoaminergic substance cannot isolate Na-channel contributions, and repeat cocaine exposure is not an ethical clinical calcium-discrimination test. [Crumb et al. sodium/channel review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2856043/).

**Expectation circuitry:** Medicine cues can initiate learned physiological effects, but the strongest direct human placebo analgesia evidence implicates opioid and dopamine systems, with other pathways and conditioning rules; a general 'serotonin controls expectations' label is not established. [Amanzio & Benedetti 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9870976/). **Sleep** includes memory-consolidation replay, homeostatic regulation, and potentially glymphatic clearance; the 'default mode network' is not synonymous with sleep replay and the entire maintenance hypothesis is not proved by fatigue or reduced subjective sleepiness. [Replay review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12998549/); [2025 clearance review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39601891/).

*No personal data; review queue only; no drug/supplement protocols.*


## Flux bottlenecks versus physical deposits: chylomicronemia, calcium phosphate and amino acids (19 October research extension)

**Reader question:** Could a failure of utilisation, export, processing or clearance cause biologically ordinary material to accumulate in a compartment, perhaps become deposited, and then impair function? Are there examples in calcium handling and amino-acid biology?

**General classification: established transport failure → compartment accumulation; conditional solubility/precipitation → some systems generate deposits; deposition → downstream functional impairment is mechanism- and model-dependent.** Do not equate circulating substrate accumulation with solid deposition, or any of these with a diagnosis in an individual.

**Lipid comparator (established, not identical):** Chylomicrons are triglyceride-rich lipoprotein particles normally hydrolysed by lipoprotein lipase (LPL), assisted by relevant cofactors/transport machinery. Chylomicronemia is persistence of these *particles in plasma*, not initially crystals or a normal depot. Multifactorial chylomicronemia predominates; rare familial syndromes result from LPL-pathway dysfunction. Eruptive xanthomas show that lipid redistribution/deposition into tissue macrophages can be a secondary consequence in severe disease. [Chait & Eckel 2019, PMID 31035285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31035285/); [classic lipid biochemical/histological/electron microscopy of chylomicron-derived xanthoma lipids, PMID 5480845](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5480845/); [2025 LPL diagnosis study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbrep.2025.102245).

**Calcium model (experimentally established ex vivo, human attribution not established):** Cytosolic free Ca2+ signals, mitochondrial *free* matrix Ca2+, matrix total calcium and precipitated calcium phosphate are different endpoints. Mitochondrial uptake/precipitation can buffer free Ca2+ and may serve protective sequestration initially. With imposed overload, isolated **guinea-pig cardiac mitochondria** showed depressed ADP-stimulated respiration linked to calcium-phosphate accumulation and complex-I-linked pathways rather than free Ca2+ alone. Proposed obstruction of NADH diffusion/cristae destabilisation was mechanistic interpretation, not directly proven sole mechanism. [2019 experimental + computational study, PMID 30615608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30615608/). Later cryo-EM directly visualised matrix calcium-phosphate granules alongside cristae/membrane disturbance in guinea-pig mitochondria loaded with calcium; temporal/structural association does not establish that deposits explain any particular human symptoms. [2021 cryo-EM, PMID 33441863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33441863/); [2022 mechanistic review, PMCID PMC9775684](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9775684/). Plasma calcium homeostasis and ordinary oral intake do not imply such mitochondrial loads; no clinical deposit, serum phosphate disorder, or SPG7 lesion is established in the individual case by these experiments.

**Amino-acid transport comparator — cystinosis (established genetic disease):** CTNS encodes lysosomal cystinosin, which uses a proton gradient to export cystine (disulfide-linked cysteine) from lysosomes. Defective export causes local cystine accumulation and can cause crystals: a clear *compartment-specific transport* failure independent of high dietary intake. [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/cystinosis/); [2024 pathophysiology review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44162-024-00041-2). **Counterexample to “deposits necessarily cause all damage”:** tubular cell injury can appear before visually detectable cystine crystals; disturbed lysosomal dynamics/autophagy, mitochondrial quality control and nutrient redistribution may contribute [Physiological Reviews 2019](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physrev.00008.2018). Do not claim crystal formation is necessary or sufficient.

**Amino-acid metabolic comparator — phenylketonuria (established metabolic accumulation; proposed assembly-mediated damage):** Deficient phenylalanine hydroxylase causes phenylalanine accumulation; a primary experimental study reported self-assembled amyloid-like *phenylalanine* fibrils at pathological concentrations, cellular toxicity, mouse hippocampal findings and antibody-reactive structures in human PKU brain tissue. The claim that such fibrils explain PKU neurological deficits remains a **hypothesis**, not settled clinical causality. [Adler-Abramovich et al 2012, PMID 22706200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22706200/). 2017 test-tube work found phenylalanine fibrils could seed aggregation of some proteins under selected conditions, but culture concentrations/conditions constrain extrapolation [PMID 28894147](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28894147/).

**Operational model for the MJB causal KG:** Encode *material + compartment + molecular form + relevant transport enzyme/channel + influx rate + efflux/clearance rate + supersaturation/buffering threshold + measured structural deposit + function/endpoints*. Do not merge distinct endpoints:
- chylomicrons *in plasma* vs triglycerides deposited in tissue macrophages;
- free calcium vs total sequestered calcium vs calcium phosphate precipitate;
- freely soluble amino-acid pool vs lysosomal cystine crystals vs pathological phenylalanine fibrils;
- aggregation of proteins composed of amino acids (proteostasis) vs direct aggregation of free amino acids.

**Three discriminating questions:** (1) Is the primary defect altered input, transporter throughput, enzyme turnover or export? (2) Is the material soluble, compartment-sequestered or structurally deposited, and which assay can distinguish those states? (3) Does measured deposition causally reduce function independently of soluble elevation, or is it initially a protective buffer/secondary marker?
