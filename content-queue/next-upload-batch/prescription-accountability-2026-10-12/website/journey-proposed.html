Dated personal account · 12 October 2026

I secured the prescription. Now comes recovery, accountability and change.

Five years of established ADHD treatment. Approximately ten months without continuity. A private prescription finally sent on 12 October 2026.

On 12 October, ADHD Direct confirmed that my new Amfexa prescription had been processed and sent by first-class post. That is the concrete change I have been trying to achieve: a clinician has accepted a treatment route and issued the prescription. At this snapshot, delivery, dispensing, restarting treatment and any improvement in my health remain to be confirmed. [C06]

I found the private route, gathered the records, paid for the appointment and worked through the clinical questions and prescription process. In my account, PIP backpay made the private route possible. This was not the result of a completed NHS prescribing handover. The prescription is an important step towards getting healthy again; it does not erase the months before it or settle the questions about what happened. [C06, C10; personal account]

Recovery is my immediate priority. Accountability and change follow, because nobody should have to perform this much coordination while losing the ability to function.

The timeline: the pathway ended, the risk continued

Before the interruption: I had been diagnosed and treated for ADHD over approximately five years. Different formulations had not felt equivalent to me; Amfexa was the formulation I considered effective. That is my treatment history and personal response, not a claim that a particular excipient explains everyone's response or that every medicine containing it is ineffective.

December 2025: CWPT's March letter records that I chose discharge on 11 December and was offered a medical review. I dispute the clinical context and what that account leaves out. The institutional position and my challenge are both retained. Whether the service ended safely, and how responsibility should have been maintained or transferred, require examination of the original clinical record. [C01, C02]

3–5 March 2026: CWPT acknowledged a new GP referral dated 3 March. Its 5 March letter refused the requested bridging prescription, citing reassessment and titration safety, and placed review in the ordinary waiting-list pathway. I am not arguing that clinicians should restart a previous high dose without assessing safety. My question is why the need for a safe reassessment did not produce a timely, owned alternative while I reported deterioration. [C01]

28 April: CWICB said my concerns were for CWPT to address and that it had contacted CWPT to ensure an appropriate response. This is documented routing and an assurance about a response, not proof that a prescriber accepted my care. [C09]

28 May: CWPT acknowledged pain, distress and feeling unheard. It listed proposed advocacy support, occupational therapy, injury follow-up, neurology and cardiology routes. It said ADHD medication could not be considered until full cardiology checks were completed. In the same letter, it asked me to contact only PALS about Trust concerns, send no more than one email per week and avoid photographs. [C03]

21 July: CWICB said it had received assurance that CWPT was taking action. It also said it had received more than 300 emails since late March, including copied correspondence, and issued a warning about possible application of its persistent/unreasonable behaviour policy. That is the ICB's stated count and characterisation, not my independent count for that period. [C12]

August: I describe this period as a crisis involving severe pain, loss of mobility and reduced capacity to manage daily life. Later CWPT correspondence confirms MHLT/HIUS escalation and engagement. On 19 August the ICB reiterated assurance from senior CWPT colleagues, stated that no single senior case owner had been assigned, and confirmed its July warning was not a formally applied contact restriction. [C07, C11; personal account]

September: I pursued a private transfer route. A receipt dated 17 September records a £450 Transfer Your Care appointment; appointment correspondence confirms 23 September at 10:30 BST. I continued providing evidence and addressing clinical questions afterwards. [C10]

6 October: CWPT correspondence said concerns could continue to be escalated, but the responding clinician's role did not allow them to identify who would resolve the medication question or when. [C07]

8–12 October: ADHD Direct issued the Amfexa treatment plan, I paid a £20 processing fee, and it confirmed prescription dispatch on 12 October. On the same morning, CWPT correspondence said my hopelessness had been discussed across services but there was no further update yet. [C06, C07, C10]

Assurances are not the same as an accepted treatment plan

Three ICB communications are directly indexed here: 28 April, 21 July and 19 August. They provide routing or assurance that CWPT would respond or was taking action. They do not each promise that a specific prescription will be issued on a specific date. This is a verified minimum set of relevant assurances, not a complete count of every time I was told something would be sorted. [C09, C11, C12]

That distinction matters. A service can say it has contacted another team. A commissioner can say it is assured. A clinician can say concerns have been escalated. The patient can still have no accepted prescribing owner, no workable interim decision and no date for the next clinical step.

The unanswered questions are practical: what action was being assured, who accepted responsibility, what timescale was agreed, what happened when it expired, and what changed for me? The 19 August statement about no single senior case owner is evidence about the ICB's arrangements at that time. It is not proof that no clinician anywhere had any responsibility. [C11]

Communication controls while the clinical question remained unresolved

I experienced some responses as irritation at my persistence. The publicly supportable wording is more precise: there are three documented correspondence chains addressing contact volume or controlling future communication. They involve CWPT, CWICB and the shared Derby & Derbyshire/Lincolnshire/Nottingham & Nottinghamshire ICB complaints team. That is at least five NHS bodies across three chains; it is not five separately imposed restrictions. [C03, C11, C12, C13]

I acknowledge the people who engaged seriously. That makes the criticism more exact: individual effort occurred, but the reviewed NHS record still does not show a completed prescribing handover before I secured the private route. Complaints handling, clinical ownership, accepting legal responsibility and learning from an incident are different measures. No institution is scored as having “accepted negligence” on the basis of an apology.

The human cost: monitored decline is not restoration

During the gap I experienced severe pain, exhaustion, posturing and loss of function. I report being unable to walk since March. Established treatment had, in my experience, enabled more automatic activity; without the medication I relied on exhausting conscious control. I was also trying to manage the executive-function demands of ADHD while organising the care required to treat it.

Those are my observations. This pack does not prove the neurological mechanism, that medication interruption caused every symptom, or that any injury is permanent. Those questions need clinical assessment and an independent review of the full record. It does show why a plan has to address the consequences of delay and non-treatment, alongside prescribing risks.

I used AI extensively to organise correspondence, compare documents, prepare questions and keep the evidence manageable. I built MJB ADHD as a resource while trying to regain continuity of my own care. That was a way of doing useful work with very limited stamina. It should never be a prerequisite for receiving coordinated care.

My experience often felt like monitored decline and apologies. I regard the prolonged failure to restore a workable route as a serious failure of care, and want the allegations of neglect investigated. That is my position, not a legal finding made by this publication.

The wider evidence: serious findings, accurately labelled

NICE recommendation 1.5.1 says: “Healthcare providers should ensure continuity of care for people with ADHD.” It supports asking for an accountable continuity plan. It does not automatically entitle every patient to a particular medicine, dose or unsafe restart. [P15]

CQC's CWPT trust-level well-led assessment, published 26 August 2026, was Good. The adult community mental-health assessment published 15 July rated Safe and Responsive as Requires improvement. The Safe report describes weaknesses in medicines systems and transitions, while also identifying positive safeguarding and incident-learning practice. The ward findings published in October 2025 concern acute/PICU wards, not an inspection of my ADHD decision. These findings provide context; they are not an adjudication of my case. [P07–P10]

The national data cards preserve their different scopes. Ministry of Justice figures show 183 deaths of people subject to Mental Health Act detention reported to coroners in England and Wales in calendar 2025. CQC separately received 314 death notifications in England in financial year 2024/25: 253 detained patients and 61 people subject to community treatment orders. These totals must not be added or labelled preventable deaths. CQC recorded 174 natural, 41 unnatural and 99 undetermined causes at reporting. [P01, P02]

The Ministry of Justice also reports 35,989 inquests opened and 654 Prevention of Future Deaths reports in 2025 in England and Wales, both across all causes. Neither is a mental-health-only or NHS-negligence count. The question this raises is how learning and implementation are checked, not how to rebrand the totals. [P01]

The Lampard statutory inquiry concerns Essex mental-health inpatient deaths in 2000–2023. The Nottinghamshire CQC special review and the Greater Manchester independent assurance review are different processes. They are not a verified national total of trusts currently under statutory investigation. [P12–P14]

What accountability needs to produce

I want a dated, evidence-based answer that separates what was known, what was decided, who had authority and what happened next. Specifically:

  1. An independent clinical timeline: discharge context, risk assessment, referral triage, prescribing decisions, cardiology requirements and inter-service communications.
  2. The evidence behind assurance: the actual actions, decision-makers, deadlines and patient outcomes that informed the ICB's reassurance.
  3. A review of communication arrangements: their scope, reasonable adjustments, review dates and preservation of a route for new clinical risk.
  4. Record accuracy: correction of established inaccuracies, annotation of disputed accounts and a clear audit trail.
  5. A sustainable treatment route: monitoring and affordability, with a documented decision on appropriate NHS funding or commissioning options.
  6. A reasoned incident/candour decision: whether the relevant reporting or candour thresholds applied, with the basis recorded rather than assumed from this narrative.
  7. Demonstrable change: responsible owner, implementation date and evidence that the redesigned pathway works for the next patient.

The local policies supplied for this project set out complaints governance and multi-agency safeguarding responsibilities. Their supplied versions are dated documents, not proof of the current intranet version or a finding of breach. The older age-limited commissioning policy is historical and must not be presented as the present eligibility rule. [L01–L03]

The change I am asking for: close the loop

MJB's proposed safeguard is simple: a referral or discharge notification should not count as a completed transfer until the receiving service has accepted responsibility, or a documented clinically appropriate alternative is in place.

The record should name the outgoing service and the accepted receiving service; identify who can make the treatment decision; explain any interim plan, including the risks of treatment and delay; give a review date; and communicate the decision accessibly to the patient. Where prescribing is declined, the patient still needs a responsible service and a workable plan. These are proposed design safeguards, not a claim that current law compels any particular prescription.

Measure time without an accepted clinical owner and an effective plan, the response to escalating risk, and whether a patient can actually use the route. Count clinical outcomes alongside meetings, letters, referrals and apologies.

I secured the private route. Now I want to recover, establish what happened and help change the system so the next patient does not have to do the same.

A01: email-count method

Two accounts were searched separately using Gmail SENT status and explicit Unix date boundaries: after 1767225599, before 1791825769. The cut-off is 12 October 2026 at 18:22:49 BST. The requested interval begins 1 January 2026 at 00:00 UTC. All pages were retrieved: 24 personal-account pages and 20 advocacy-account pages, with returned pages of up to 100 IDs despite a higher requested limit.

Repeated Gmail IDs were removed within each account. Result: 2,368 + 1,988 = 4,356 retained SENT message records. No estimate supplied by Gmail was used as the count. Cross-account RFC Message-ID deduplication and a full content classification into care-seeking versus wider outreach were not performed. Deleted or unavailable messages may not be represented. Recipient multiplicity does not multiply the count.

Publish as “4,356 sent-folder message records across two accounts in 2026, including wider correspondence.” Do not shorten to “4,356 ignored requests for treatment.” The private audit retains query, IDs and completion state for reproducibility without disclosing that material publicly.

Date and hour calculations

Calendar differences to 12 October 2026: 11 December 2025 = 305 days; 5 March = 221 days; 28 April = 167 days; 19 August = 54 days. Counts use ordinary date subtraction, excluding the starting day. 305 × 24 = 7,320 nominal calendar hours, not logged work or a precisely timed clinical-event interval.

Private appointment: 23 September 2026 10:30 BST = 09:30 UTC. Dispatch email: 12 October 2026 11:29:09 BST = 10:29:09 UTC. Difference = 456 hours 59 minutes 9 seconds, rounded to about 457 elapsed hours. This includes nights and weekends. Patient-reported 2 December interruption yields 314 days / 7,536 nominal calendar hours; kept separate from the recorded discharge date.
