# Accessible therapeutic alternatives & support: when prescribed care is interrupted

*Editorial draft — NOT published or medical advice. Research reviewed 19 October 2026. For public use, commission clinical/pharmacy review of interaction statements, UK availability and link accessibility before integration.*

## A different question from “which supplement is strongest?”

What can someone realistically access **while an effective prescription, assessment or follow-up is unavailable**? The answer must separate four things: (1) a **route to restore prescribed treatment**; (2) a **self-care measure with human outcome evidence**; (3) **correction of a specific deficiency**; and (4) a **pharmacologically active but clinically risky substance**. One person may need several of these, but they are not interchangeable. The fact that a plant contains a neurotransmitter precursor does not establish which symptoms it treats, whether it crosses biological barriers in sufficient concentrations or whether its commercial preparations are consistent.

Where medicines have been interrupted, **the first priority remains restoring an appropriately assessed prescription and a named clinical owner**. In England, NHS 111's online emergency-prescription service may help for an eligible regularly prescribed repeat medicine, and GP/out-of-hours or urgent care routes exist [A1]. It **does not provide Schedule 2/3 controlled drugs by ordinary pharmacy emergency-supply mechanisms**, including commonly prescribed controlled ADHD stimulants [A2]. This matters especially because “take a supplement instead” is not an acceptable continuity-of-care plan.

## Three starting examples: what is actually known?

| Option and intended goal | Human evidence and real endpoint | What the finding cannot establish | Practical caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mucuna pruriens**, a levodopa-containing plant; investigation mainly in Parkinson's motor symptoms | A 2025 randomised single-blind crossover experiment (11 evaluable participants) found higher levodopa drug exposure and a longer clinically rated ON period than levodopa/benserazide in its tested doses. A separate 12-month open-label phase-2 trial randomised 35 untreated Parkinson patients and reported broadly comparable clinical outcomes with a *characterised preparation* [M1, M2]. | Neither trial establishes usefulness for ADHD, depression, unrelated “low dopamine,” spasticity or unassessed motor symptoms. Pharmacokinetic exposure is not a general measure of functional benefit. | It supplies a **real drug precursor**, not merely nutrients. In 16 sampled US retail products, levodopa was absent in one and measured at 2–241 mg per serving in the other 15; no carbidopa or benserazide was detected [M3]. Unpredictable exposure, nausea, blood-pressure effects, dyskinesia and medicine interactions demand clinical supervision. |
| **Saffron (Crocus sativus)**; depression/anxiety support, preliminary ADHD research | A 2026 GRADE meta-analysis (34 RCTs; 1,769 adults) found improvement in self-reported Beck depression and anxiety scales, but **not** significant improvement in clinician-rated Hamilton scales; heterogeneity on the positive outcomes was very high (I² >92%) [S1]. A six-week adult ADHD adjunct trial randomised 56 people receiving methylphenidate; 44 completed, with an ASRS signal but not a statistically significant CAARS between-group change [S2]. | Not proved **better than standard antidepressants**, not proved a replacement for ADHD stimulants and not demonstrated to act primarily through a dopamine mechanism in treated humans. A 2019 active-comparator ADHD pilot in 54 children failed to identify a difference; this was **not an equivalence trial** [S3]. | Check product extract and interactions with antidepressants or other neuroactive drugs. Pregnancy, bipolar vulnerability and long-term safety need specific assessment. Do not silently combine with prescribed psychotropic medication. |
| **Montmorency tart cherry**, juice/concentrate; possible sleep-duration or sleep-quality support | A 2012 blinded crossover trial in 20 volunteers found higher urinary melatonin metabolite and better actigraphy-based sleep-duration/efficiency measurements over seven days [T1]. A 2025 systematic review identified six clinical studies, five reporting some significant sleep benefit [T2]. | Cherries are **not dose-equivalent to a medicinal melatonin formulation**. Natural melatonin varies with product and is not proven to account for every observed effect. Small trials, short follow-up, different populations and preparations limit confidence. | Concentrated juice may add sugar and acidity; extracts are not necessarily equivalent to tested juice preparations. Persistent insomnia, circadian disturbance or sleep-disordered breathing needs appropriate assessment. |

**Source interpretation:** Saffron's *clinical mood evidence* is more developed than its proposed precise monoamine mechanism. Mucuna's *biochemical identity* is clearer than any claim of broad psychiatric or motor benefit. Tart cherry's *sleep signal* is more meaningful than calling it a replacement “melatonin tablet made from fruit.” Do not merge mechanisms or count the same trial multiple times across reviews.

## More candidates worth organising, without a misleading “natural pharmacy” league table

### Plausible limited-support options

- **L-theanine (sleep/stress):** a 2025 meta-analysis of 19 papers (897 participants; 18 pooled) found small/moderate improvements in some *subjective* sleep measures, but not corresponding clear improvements in objective sleep duration or efficiency. Many studies included additional ingredients, so attribution is uncertain [O1]. **Evidence: emerging, generally low-to-moderate certainty for the targeted outcomes.**
- **Standardised oral lavender oil (Silexan; anxiety):** a pooled analysis of five blinded trials (1,213 randomised) found better clinician-rated and self-rated anxiety outcomes versus placebo over ten weeks [O2]. **Evidence: moderate for studied formulations/indications, not general lavender oil or ADHD treatment.** Essential oils sold for fragrance are not automatically ingestible or equivalent.
- **Iron when iron-deficient; vitamin B12 when deficient (symptom-mimic correction):** addressing an established deficiency can matter far more than using a neuroactive supplement indiscriminately. NHS advice links iron deficiency to restless-legs symptoms, and NICE identifies B12 deficiency as a possible cause of balance, gait, proprioceptive and cognitive symptoms [O3, O4]. **Evidence: established for correction of deficiency, not evidence that indiscriminate supplementation treats ADHD or neurological disease.**
- **Omega-3 EPA/DHA (nutrition and exploratory ADHD support):** evidence for ADHD substitution is weak: a Cochrane review of children's trials found little or no overall improvement versus placebo [O5]. Different indications must be evaluated separately. **Evidence: no convincing benefit as ADHD replacement.**
- **Magnesium, L-tyrosine and creatine:** biologically relevant to ion function, catecholamine synthesis and cellular energy respectively, but this is **not direct evidence of useful replacement treatment** for ADHD, depression, spasticity or dopamine-responsive disorders. Consider deficiency-specific assessments and clearly label any broader use as unproven. **Evidence for those replacement claims: insufficient.**

### Important “active does not mean safer” entries

- **St John's wort:** may help mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms in some studies, but creates major enzyme/transporter interactions, can reduce the action of other medicines and can cause dangerous serotonergic interactions [R1]. It belongs in a **high-interaction / discuss with clinician** category, not a simple OTC recommendation.
- **Ashwagandha:** small/short-term sleep or stress signals do not remove reports of rare liver injury, possible thyroid effects and interactions with sedatives or other medicines [R2].
- **5-HTP / L-tryptophan / SAMe, high-dose multi-ingredient “neurotransmitter boosters”:** should **not** be promoted as antidepressant substitutes, especially if serotonergic medicines are involved, without product-specific human evidence and interaction review. Mechanistic marketing is not a safety assessment.
- **Valerian, lemon balm, passionflower, glycine, rhodiola, NAC and similar candidates:** possible *future individual evidence cards*, not endorsed alternatives. Require intervention-specific endpoints, safety and interaction review before inclusion in public-facing choice tables.

## How readers should compare options

Ask these six questions before comparing products:

1. **Target:** ADHD executive function, depressed mood, anxiety, insomnia, pain, motor control and correction of deficiency are separate clinical questions.
2. **Endpoint:** Was benefit measured in actigraphy, clinician-rated scales, real-world daily functioning, a blood concentration, or only self-report?
3. **Population:** Was it studied in adults with the relevant condition, healthy volunteers, children, or people with Parkinson's disease?
4. **Preparation:** Was the retail product standardised, independently assayed and comparable to the trial material?
5. **Duration:** Short pilot results do not demonstrate chronic safety, sustained efficacy or disease modification.
6. **Interaction/access:** Could this interact with ongoing prescriptions, and is the NHS care-continuity pathway being pursued concurrently?

UK NHS guidance notes that herbal medicines are not tested as standard medicines are. A **Traditional Herbal Registration (THR)** indicates assessed quality/safety and evidence of *traditional use for minor conditions*, **not** proof of efficacy for serious psychiatric or neurological disease [A3].

**Clinical boundary:** New loss of walking ability, progressive asymmetrical neurological findings, severe spasticity, altered consciousness or acute psychiatric crisis require timely clinical assessment. Self-selected supplements must never be offered as a substitute for that assessment or for continuity of prescribed care.
