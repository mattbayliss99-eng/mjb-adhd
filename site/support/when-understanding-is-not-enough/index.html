When understanding is not enough

Matthew Bayliss · 14 October 2026

About this account: This describes my experience, not a definition of every person's ADHD or a clinical assessment.

I can understand the whole task and still lose the next step

“Executive dysfunction” sounds much tidier than it feels.

I can understand a task, explain why it matters and work out how to do it. The difficulty comes when I have to keep the next action available long enough to start it, while pain, discomfort and distractions compete for the same limited attention.

By the time I can initiate the action, I may have forgotten what it was.

Then I have to reconstruct it. Find the instructions again. Work out where I stopped. Recover the intention. Try to begin. All of that is work, but from outside it can look as though nothing has happened.

One active step is often all I can carry

For me, a useful instruction looks like this:

  1. Open this named file.
  2. Wait for this specific screen.
  3. Press this labelled button.

Each step needs to remain visible while I do it. I need to be able to return after an interruption and see exactly where I am.

If I try to hold the whole plan in my head, I can lose both the plan and the action I was about to take. Sometimes two tightly connected actions fit together. More than that can mean standing there trying to remember everything and ending up with none of it available.

Making instructions explicit saves me from repeatedly rebuilding the task. Something being obvious does not make it reliably accessible at the moment I need it.

Pain makes the usable window smaller

These difficulties happen even when I am lying down. Sitting at a computer adds discomfort and the effort of maintaining a position. My attention and endurance can become much worse.

I described it as being useful for perhaps one minute out of every five. That is an estimate of how it feels, not a measured performance score. It captures the mismatch between the time I spend trying and the time in which I can actually move the task forward.

A short setup can consume a long stretch of my day. The gaps between actions contain pain, distraction, lost intentions and repeated attempts to recover my place.

Thinking and communicating have different costs

My internal thinking can feel fast, connected and stimulating. Speaking requires me to turn that into a sequence someone else can follow, while listening, holding their contribution in mind and managing my body at the same time.

Sometimes I have plenty I could say, but very little capacity left to say it. In conversations I cannot sustain, the words can become background noise while a tune or an internal thought stream takes over. Fighting to stay engaged can itself become exhausting.

Being quiet, flat or apparently uninterested does not reliably tell someone how interested I am, how much I understand or how hard I am trying. I have been criticised for seeming miserable or uncommunicative while already struggling with pain and reduced access to things I found helpful.

I experienced Amfexa as making it easier to participate and express myself, rather than spending so much effort manually getting through activity. That is my account of its benefit, not a recommendation about anyone else's medication or dose.

Depth of understanding does not guarantee consistent output

I can follow a complicated mechanism and make useful connections, yet struggle to retrieve a familiar word, organise an explanation or retain the next action. My writing may vary greatly in structure and clarity as my available capacity changes.

The knowledge and the ability to present or act on it do not always arrive together. A fluent explanation on one occasion does not mean that capacity will be available on demand later.

What makes a practical difference

  • Show one active step, with the exact filename or button label.
  • Keep my place visible and save progress automatically.
  • Make it easy to stop and return without reconstructing the whole task.
  • Let long processes continue without requiring my attention or an open conversation.
  • Give me a clear result and one explicit next action.

These changes reduce the amount I have to remember, recover and repeatedly initiate. They let more of my limited effort reach the task itself.
