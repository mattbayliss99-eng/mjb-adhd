Medications & Supplements

Medications & Supplements

Last updated 1 October 2026

Treatment can be life-changing — for better or worse. ADHD is not a single uniform mechanism, and the same diagnosis can hide very different underlying response patterns. That is one reason why people can react so differently to the same medication.

This page is a practical guide to medication types, response patterns, medical cannabis, and possible adjuncts. It is not a substitute for personal medical advice.

1. Medication types

Amphetamine-based stimulants

Examples: dexamfetamine, lisdexamfetamine.

Broad mechanism: increase the functional availability of dopamine and noradrenaline. Often particularly useful where the main difficulty is initiation, under-arousal, low drive, poor task-starting, and “I know what to do but cannot begin.”

Strengths: strong effect on task initiation; can help motivation, mental energy and “getting going”; may improve attention, reward salience and throughput.

Limitations: can cause physical tension, appetite loss, sleep issues, agitation or overstimulation; can feel “too sharp” for some people.

Practical note: a strong positive response can suggest that increasing functional catecholamine availability is highly relevant for that person.

Methylphenidate-based stimulants

Examples: methylphenidate immediate-release and modified-release forms.

Broad mechanism: mainly reduces dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake, helping those signals persist longer.

Strengths: can improve focus, task engagement and distractibility; some people find it smoother or easier to tolerate than amphetamine-based options.

Limitations: for some people it improves focus without truly fixing initiation; can still cause appetite suppression, anxiety, crash effects or tension.

Practical note: someone may respond well for attention but still need a different option if the core problem is “can focus once moving, but cannot reliably start.”

Atomoxetine

Broad mechanism: a non-stimulant that mainly increases noradrenaline signalling over time.

Strengths: may help steadiness, emotional regulation and baseline control; sometimes useful where stimulants are poorly tolerated.

Limitations: slower onset than stimulants; often less dramatic in day-to-day feel; can cause nausea, fatigue, dizziness or mood effects.

Practical note: useful for some people, but not a like-for-like replacement for a stimulant response.

Guanfacine

Broad mechanism: a non-stimulant that can affect prefrontal control networks and arousal regulation.

Strengths: can reduce over-reactivity or help calmness; sometimes helpful for sleep, emotional volatility or hyperarousal patterns.

Limitations: can cause sedation, low blood pressure, dizziness or fatigue; may improve regulation without meaningfully improving initiation.

Key principle. Never assume one failed medication means all treatment will fail. Different drugs can have radically different outcomes in the same person. If the fit is poor, that may be a reason to keep investigating — not to give up.

2. How ADHD medications actually work

ADHD medications do not “create discipline.” They change the operating conditions of brain networks involved in attention, initiation, reward, task persistence, arousal, emotional regulation and, in some people, motor timing or physical tension.

Two people can share an ADHD diagnosis while having very different dominant bottlenecks: one may mainly struggle with attention drift, another with initiation failure, another with emotional volatility or under-arousal, another with a mixture of all three. That is why the same medicine can feel transformative for one person and poor for another.

3. What your response pattern may be telling you

Helps focus but not initiation

Possible meaning: attention improves once engaged, but the task-starting bottleneck remains.

Ask: is the medication improving concentration without adequately improving activation?

Helps motivation but causes agitation or tension

Possible meaning: activation may be increasing, but the dose or fit may be poor.

Ask: dose, timing, release profile or medication family may need review.

Makes you calmer but not more functional

Possible meaning: regulation improved more than activation.

Ask: is treatment helping emotional steadiness without solving the core executive problem?

Works briefly then drops off

Possible meaning: duration may be wrong, coverage incomplete, or the formulation may not fit.

Ask: about timing, duration, split dosing or alternative release forms.

Causes emotional flattening

Possible meaning: the treatment may be overshooting or reducing spontaneity too much.

Ask: about dose, family, or whether a non-stimulant/stimulant mix discussion is needed.

Helps cognition but worsens sleep

Possible meaning: beneficial overall but mistimed or too activating.

Ask: review schedule, cutoff time and overall sleep strategy.

4. Medical cannabis

Medical cannabis is available in different forms, including flower and oil. In lived-experience terms, these can feel very different.

Personal recommendation: flower rather than oil, used via dry herb vaporising.

  • No rolling required.
  • Easier if hands are shaky, dexterity is poor, or executive dysfunction makes preparation harder.
  • Avoids combustion.
  • Can offer a more direct, controllable practical routine.
  • Medical supply can provide more consistency of access and strain profile than informal acquisition.
  • Can be especially useful for people who are unwell, housebound or mobility-limited.

This is practical/personal experience, not a clinical trial result and not universal advice.

Safety note. Cannabis is not for everyone. It can worsen anxiety, cognition, motivation or functioning in some people. Sedation, interaction with driving rules and broader legal/prescribing issues all matter.

Go deeper. Medical cannabis in depth covers cannabinoids, terpenes, vaporisation and uncertainty. Strain & chemovar reviews keeps personal response apart from product data.

6. Serious warning

ADHD treatment can alter the course of a person’s life for better or worse. Do not assume every service will protect you simply because it says it exists to help.

  • Record appointments.
  • Save every letter and email.
  • Keep copies of prescriptions, refusals, delays and outcome statements.
  • Document deterioration, side effects and functional impact.

Different medications can produce radically different outcomes. If a treatment is clearly wrong for you, do not assume that means all treatment will be wrong. Do not settle too early if the result is poor.

Blind trust is dangerous. Administrative pressure, delay or cost considerations may not align with your personal health needs. Protect yourself, keep records, and take treatment decisions seriously.

7. Final warning: this is what being left looks like

This is the amount of pills I have to take without my ADHD medication. Even with all of these, I cannot walk, I have basically no memory or executive function, and low energy and pain compound everything until doing anything at all becomes impossible.

This is the state the NHS can leave you in — and not care in the slightest.

For your own safety: keep all correspondence and expect nothing. You can only rely on family and friends to support you; currently the NHS is not reliable. Too many people die because of people who could not care less.

If you or someone you know is in immediate danger, call 999. Urgent but not an emergency: NHS 111. Samaritans: 116 123.
