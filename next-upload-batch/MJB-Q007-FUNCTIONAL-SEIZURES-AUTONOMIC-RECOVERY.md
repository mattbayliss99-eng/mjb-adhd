# MJB-Q007 — Functional seizures: autonomic physiology, the recovery period, and testable threshold models

**Status:** evidence scoping / unpublished editorial candidate  
**Date:** 2026-10-12  
**Integration preference:** expand existing FND guide (`guidebook/content/pages/70-fnd.md`) and link the separately staged paediatric action-plan concept MJB-Q006; do not create a competing generic FND page.

## Core research question
What is established about sympathetic/autonomic activation before, during and after **functional/dissociative seizures (FDS)**; what happens during recovery; and which alternative causal threshold models could be evaluated without claiming a proven catecholamine depletion mechanism?

## Evidence summary, graded

### A. Meaningful recovery symptoms — observational support
A 2024 two-hospital seizure-semiology study reported post-event symptoms in 72.3% of participants with functional seizures; confusion/fatigue in 57.8%; speech/language disturbance in 21.5%; headache in 18.6%; and rapid recovery in 21.8%. These are sample proportions, not diagnostic tests and not proof of a fixed number of hours or neurotransmitter depletion. They justify explicit recording of **recovery as part of the episode burden**, rather than only seizure count.
Source: *Functional seizure semiology and classification in a public and private hospital*, Seizure 122 (2024) 71–79, DOI 10.1016/j.seizure.2024.09.020, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059131124002681 .

### B. Peri-event autonomic shifts — small physiological study
Romigi and colleagues examined HRV across baseline, preictal, ictal and immediate postictal periods in **22** people undergoing video-EEG monitoring (11 only FDS, 11 FDS plus epilepsy). Their measured HRV changes were compatible with greater cardiac sympathetic activation before/during episodes and greater vagal activity afterwards. The groups were small, supine, awake and selected for artifact-free recordings; analysis was around ~2-minute segments and HRV metrics are imperfect proxies for sympathetic/vagal input. This does not directly quantify noradrenaline release, dopamine, ATP reserves or bloodstream catecholamines and cannot establish a universal causal pathway.
Source: Romigi et al., *Frontiers in Neurology* (2020), PMID 32849194, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32849194/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7426492/ .

### C. Heterogeneity and competing diagnoses
The American Academy of Neurology's functional seizure practice guideline recommends accurate descriptions, witness accounts/video and video-EEG when useful, plus assessment for **coexisting epilepsy** and psychiatric comorbidities. Do not assume every attack or extreme physiological reading is functional because the person has an FND diagnosis. An extreme blood-pressure/heart-rate episode requires appropriate acute medical assessment; device artifacts and motion can distort readings but are not a reason to ignore them.
Source: *Management of Functional Seizures*, AAN, Neurology 106 (2026), DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000214466, PMID 41370742, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41370742/ .

### D. What patients report helping — hypothesis-generating survey
A 2026 study of **244 respondents**, recruited via international social media/patient groups plus a specialty clinic, reported grounding/sensory strategies (46%), practical home/school/work changes (41%), breath work (31%), and movement/exercise (27%) among most helpful approaches. Selection, recall, confounding and self-report bias preclude causal inference. These data suggest practical questions for action plans and controlled studies, not guaranteed treatments.
Source: *What helps? Patient-reported strategies for managing functional/dissociative seizures*, *Epilepsy & Behavior* 182 (2026) 111105, DOI 10.1016/j.yebeh.2026.111105, PMID 42161150, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42161150/ .

## Hypotheses that need **testing**, NOT website-level assertions
1. **Autonomic arousal threshold:** increasing arousal and inadequate compensatory buffering precede an episode. Alternative: autonomic changes can be consequences, correlated outputs or anticipatory reactions.
2. **Feedback delay / instability:** delayed or unreliable state estimation, over-correction or altered coupling in central/autonomic networks could produce abrupt state transitions. Control-theory analogy is not a validated individual diagnosis.
3. **Metabolic or ionic reserve:** energetic sufficiency, lactate, glucose, electrolytes, intracellular calcium, channel conductances, astrocyte function or other state variables might affect recovery. No particular factor has been shown to be the common FDS trigger.
4. **Monoamine resource-shift model:** a proposed idea that a change between dopamine- and noradrenaline-dominant modes exhausts a distinct shared resource. **No direct clinical evidence established for FDS**; avoid presenting this as a known chemical 'blue-screen' mechanism. Test would require direct measures, specificity, temporal precedence and separation from arousal/stress effects.
5. **Shared failure topology across diagnoses:** epilepsy, FDS, panic, ADHD and bipolar disorder could share some abstract properties (thresholds, autonomic arousal, feedback, sleep interactions) while differing markedly in physiology and diagnostic definition. Analogy does NOT imply one universal transmitter fault. Bipolar biology includes polygenic susceptibility, circadian rhythms, calcium homeostasis, mitochondrial and inflammatory pathways; role and causality vary by study.

Bipolar references: Scott & McClung, *Curr Opin Neurobiol* (2023), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10786345/ ; McCarthy et al., *Bipolar Disorders* (2022), DOI 10.1111/bdi.13165, PMID 34850507, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34850507/ .

## Proposed data model for a practical guide / observational study
**Separate 5 phases**: ordinary baseline -> prodrome -> event -> immediate recovery (minutes) -> later recovery (hours or days). Capture event semiology and duration; verified episode type where possible; ECG HR/HRV, BP measured correctly, respiration, oxygen saturation when clinically appropriate; medication timing; sleep/exertion/temperature; pain, cognitive fog, muscle ache, speech and functional capacity after the event; intervention effects; and adverse events. Avoid asking patients to provoke attacks or change medication.

Distinguish changes **before** the attack (possible predictor/trigger), **during** (possible component/consequence), and **after** (possible recovery marker). Record objective trends and first-person experience side by side. No single wearable metric proves neurochemical depletion.

## Social summary candidate (not posted)
“A functional seizure can have a significant aftermath, not just an event and immediate reset. Studies report post-episode fatigue/confusion and measurable changes in autonomic state, though the specific mechanisms remain uncertain. Better assessment and episode plans should measure both attacks and recovery, and urgent abnormal vital signs must never be dismissed because someone has FND.”
