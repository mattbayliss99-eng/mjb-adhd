# MJB-Q006 — Functional seizures in teenagers: practical prevention, episode plans and returning to valued activities

**Status:** research-backed editorial draft; unpublished  
**Captured:** 2026-10-11  
**Proposed destination:** extend existing `guidebook/content/pages/70-fnd.md` and optionally create a shorter linked caregiver/school action-plan resource  
**User approval:** required before integration and public publishing  
**Audience:** parents, teens, schools, activity leaders, clinicians  
**Trigger:** American caregiver report of teenager with functional seizures, panic attacks, psychiatric medication and increased episodes during colour guard, despite being averse to previous talk therapy.

## Short answer
Functional seizures are genuine involuntary neurological events. Helping a teenager remain involved in meaningful activities, where safe, is a legitimate goal. Repeated attacks require a neurological/medication review and an individual safety plan; an FND diagnosis does not exclude coexisting epilepsy or other medical triggers.

## Practical pathway
1. **Review diagnosis and medication purpose.** Ask which medicines are prescribed, which condition each targets (e.g., anxiety/panic, epilepsy if also diagnosed, sleep), whether there has been a measured response, and whether worsening frequency merits reassessment. Avoid suggesting stopping prescribed medicines independently. Video-EEG of representative episodes may be useful where appropriate; smartphone videos only with permission.
2. **Agree an individualised episode plan** with the specialist and teenager, shared with a designated colour-guard coach/school nurse: warning signs, when to move away from flags/replica equipment, hazards to avoid, how to call for help, consent-based reassurance, episode duration and emergency thresholds. Reduce spectators and unnecessary stimulation. Do not hold down the person, insert anything into their mouth, or try to force them to 'snap out of it.' Following an episode, check alertness, injury and recovery.
3. **Identify modifiable aggravating factors, not presupposed causes.** For rehearsals record timing, heat/humidity, exertion, sleep, food/drink, performance demands, fatigue, medication timing and recovery. Use structured breaks, access to cool/quiet space, pacing and modified choreography as appropriate. Don't equate 'more episodes during colour guard' with 'colour guard causes FND'; and don't automatically remove valued activity.
4. **Trial grounded coping skills with assent and practice when well.** For a warning phase, try a pre-agreed grounded focus, counting exhalations, regular gentle breathing, orienting to a stable visual/tactile anchor, or an individually trained competing response. No technique is universally effective; avoid enforced breathing or confrontation. Post-episode: predictable low-stimulation recovery, hydration and food when alert, gradual return as tolerated. Record episode counts, duration, injuries, fatigue and attendance/function over time.
5. **Treatment adaptation:** reluctance to engage with supportive talk therapy does not exclude specific motor/behavioural retraining. Ask about ReACT (Retraining and Control Therapy), a structured symptom-focused paediatric intervention with promising *small* trials. Specialist input and accessible remote provision may be considered. The 2020 RCT enrolled 32 randomised, 29 evaluated at primary endpoint, reported 100% episode-free at seven days in ReACT participants and 82% at 60 days; limitations include small sample, selective inclusion, brief follow-up and no 60-day comparison data for supportive therapy. A 2025 uncontrolled telehealth cohort of 34 adolescents reported 87% with at least 50% episode reduction at 6 months; no contemporaneous control and author commercial conflicts must be disclosed.
6. **Emergency rule:** treat a first, different, unexplained, injury-associated or breathing-compromised event as a potential emergency; follow the personal seizure action plan and local emergency rules. Do not presume every episode is functional. When the nature of the attack is unknown, standard seizure first aid applies.

## Communication and stigma
Use 'functional seizures' rather than putting 'seizure-like' in sceptical-looking quotation marks, but do not infer that a worried parent using familiar terminology intends to belittle their child. Explicitly affirm that attacks are real and involuntary. Also avoid depicting general or specialist psychotherapy as universally futile: assess the intervention *actually tried*, its targets, barriers and alternatives.

## Supplement evidence: saffron, ashwagandha, taurine, magnesium L-threonate
No reliable paediatric functional-seizure prevention evidence for these supplements was identified in the sources reviewed for this draft. An adult-only 2026 meta-analysis of saffron for anxiety/depression does not demonstrate effectiveness for functional seizures in teenagers. Ashwagandha has limited and preparation-dependent stress/sleep evidence, potential interactions with sedatives, anticonvulsants and thyroid drugs and rare reports of liver injury. Magnesium is physiologically essential, but no proof magnesium L-threonate prevents functional seizures; supplementation is normally appropriate when dietary inadequacy/deficiency or another medical indication is established. Taurine's mechanistic interest does not translate to demonstrated paediatric FND efficacy. Because this case involves a minor taking psychiatric medications, do not recommend an unsupervised stack before medication, age, interaction and underlying conditions review.

## Social reply draft (for editing)
"Could I ask which medications she's on, and what each was prescribed for? Saying they 'help' doesn't necessarily mean the functional seizures are adequately controlled. Since episodes are becoming more frequent with colour guard, I'd ask her neurology team to review the diagnosis, medication response and an individual written episode plan for her and her coach. Look for patterns in heat, exertion, sleep, breaks, sensory load and recovery, without assuming she must quit something she loves. During an episode, keep her safe, clear hazards, remain calm, time it, and follow her medical plan rather than trying to force her to snap out of it. If ordinary talking therapy hasn't suited her, look up **ReACT — Retraining and Control Therapy**: it's more structured and symptom-focused, and has encouraging early research in teenagers. Functional seizures are real, involuntary events. New or different attacks and injuries still deserve urgent medical assessment. I'd also review any supplements with her prescriber before adding them, especially with psychiatric medications."
